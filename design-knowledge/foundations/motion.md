# Motion

Covers what moves in an interface and why: the job of each animation, how long it lasts and how it eases, how things appear, leave and change place, motion that plays by itself, motion tied to scrolling, and reduced motion. Not covered here: which feedback an action gets and when a wait shows progress, and content that moves the layout while a page loads.

- These rules are a floor: meeting them all makes a product well made, not yet good.
- **Every product:** holds even when popular products break it, and no product's own rules change it.
- **Current practice:** what research, the design systems of top products and strong designers recommend now. A product's own documented value replaces it.
- **Default:** a starting point for designing, taken from strong designers' practice: a review finding only when a harm can be named.
- **Fail and Pass:** examples. A Pass shows one way to meet the rule, not the only one.

The rules come in four groups: rules for every level, then rules for primitives (one animation: its length, its curve, what it animates), components (a menu, dialog or panel entering and leaving, content that changes in place, and items that move together) and pages (scrolling).

## Every level

### 1. Motion has a job, and frequent actions get little or none

Each animation does one of three jobs: it shows what a press did, where something came from or went, or how two states connect. Motion that does none of these goes, except at a few chosen moments, such as the first screen of a landing page or a first-run step. When a page loads, its content shows at once, or fades or staggers in within about half a second; it never waits on a slow entrance. The more often people see a motion, the shorter and quieter it is: actions repeated many times a day, and menus or popovers opened from the keyboard, change at once or within about 150 ms. A popup opened from the keyboard takes typing from its first frame, and one closed with Escape disappears at once. Charts and counters move only when their values change, never replaying from zero on each load.

- **Why:** Animation helps people follow what moved and keep their place (Bederson and Boltman 1999; Heer and Robertson 2007), adds little when a still view shows the same thing (Tversky et al. 2002), and makes people wait, which they disliked even when it made them more accurate (Baudisch et al. 2006). The more often people see a motion, the more that costs: Atlassian keeps motion repeated dozens of times a day under 150 ms and saves richer motion for brand moments such as onboarding, Apple, Carbon and Material 3 cut motion on frequent actions, and Base UI skips the animation of a tooltip shown by keyboard focus and of a popup dismissed with Escape (Atlassian, "Motion"; Apple, "Motion"; Carbon, "Motion"; Material 3, "Applying transitions"; Base UI, "Tooltip" and "Context Menu"). Motion on a keyboard action only delays people who are already typing (Emil Kowalski, "Design Engineering"), and data that replays from zero on every load shows no change at all (Emil Kowalski, "Removing Animations"). Content entering on load finishes within 500 ms (Carbon, "Choreography"; Primer Primitives, motion tokens).
- **Kind:** Current practice: every animation has one of these jobs; page content on load shows at once or finishes fading or staggering in within about 500 ms, apart from a few chosen moments; motion on frequent actions and on popups opened from the keyboard lasts about 150 ms or less, or there is none; a popup opened from the keyboard takes typing from its first frame, and one closed with Escape disappears at once; charts and counters animate only a change in value.
- **Applies when:** something animates.
- **Check:** open the DevTools Animations panel, use the screen, and list each animation group with what starts it and how often people meet it. Name each one's job: one with no job fails, unless it is one of a few chosen moments. Reload the page with the cache off (Network panel, "Disable cache"): content still entering more than about 500 ms after it could show, on every load, fails, apart from one chosen moment. Time the frequent animations (hover, press, selecting a row, menus, tabs) and the menus and popovers opened from the keyboard: over about 150 ms fails. Open each command menu or popover from the keyboard and type at once: a keystroke lost to its entrance fails. Close each popup with Escape: one that animates out fails. Reload a dashboard: charts or counters that grow from zero again fail.
- **Fail:** a 400 ms slide on every tab switch. Every section of a dashboard fading up one after another over two seconds on each visit. A command menu that zooms in each time it opens. A switch that slides to On each time the page loads. Bars that grow from zero on every load and every refresh.
- **Pass:** tabs that switch at once, with a 150 ms fade of the panel. A command menu that appears at once. A landing page whose first screen animates in once.
- **Not a fail:** a slower, richer entrance on a landing page's first screen, or on an onboarding step that people see once, when it plays in space kept for it, so nothing around it moves.

### 2. Motion is never the only way to say something, and content never waits for it

What a motion tells people is also there without it: a shaking field comes with a message, a spinner with a busy state that screen readers get, a moved item with its new place. Content is visible at rest. If an entrance animation never runs, because a script failed or the person asked for less motion, nothing stays hidden.

- **Why:** People who miss a motion, or have turned motion off, lose what only the motion said, so WCAG asks for input errors in text and for status messages, such as a busy state, that screen readers get (WCAG 2.2, 3.3.1 and 4.1.3), and Apple, Carbon and Primer ask for the same message without motion (Apple, "Motion"; Carbon, "Motion"; Primer, "Motion and animation"). Content left at `opacity: 0` until a script or a scroll position reveals it stays invisible when that never happens, and content that faded in on scroll made some people think the page had ended, because the space below looked empty (Paul, "Scroll Fading 101", 2023).
- **Kind:** Every product: information that motion carries is also shown without it, since people who miss the motion, or have turned it off, lose it otherwise (for errors and status messages, WCAG 2.2, 3.3.1 and 4.1.3); content is visible when its animation doesn't run, since content left invisible can't be read. Current practice: no reveal that leaves empty space where content will appear.
- **Applies when:** something animates.
- **Check:** in DevTools, open the Rendering panel and emulate `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`. Repeat each action and scroll the whole page: anything you could tell only from motion before fails, and so does content that stays invisible. In the Elements panel, find content set to `opacity: 0` or `visibility: hidden` at rest and revealed by a class or a script; a reveal that leaves blank space below the fold as you scroll fails current practice.
- **Fail:** an error shown only by shaking the field. Sections at `opacity: 0` until an observer adds a class, which stay blank under reduced motion.
- **Pass:** a shake together with a message under the field. Sections visible from the start, with a short fade added only when motion is allowed.
- **Not a fail:** a spinner that stops turning under reduced motion while its "Loading" label stays.

### 3. People who ask for less motion get it

When the system asks for reduced motion (`prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`), parallax, movement tied to scrolling, zooming and scaling, movement across large areas and motion that plays by itself stop, or turn into a quick fade. Smaller movement turns into a fade or an instant change. Feedback stays: a press still shows, a panel still appears, progress still shows, and motion that follows the person's own finger or pointer still follows it. A product with a lot of motion, or with animated images people post, also has its own setting for less motion, which starts from the system's.

- **Why:** Motion on screen can bring on nausea and migraine in people with vestibular disorders, who may need bed rest to recover (WCAG 2.2, Understanding 2.3.3); moving or busy scenes make a long-lasting kind of dizziness worse (Staab et al. 2017), and about 5% of adults have vestibular vertigo in a year (Neuhauser et al. 2005). WCAG counts movement, change of size and, since 2025, blurring as motion, but not changes of opacity or color, so a fade is an allowed replacement (Understanding 2.3.3; WCAG 2.2 errata). The setting asks for such motion to be removed or replaced (Media Queries 5): Apple and Material 3 turn slides and zooms into fades, Apple keeps motion that follows a gesture, and Atlassian and Primer turn motion off (Apple, "Accessibility"; Material 3, "Applying transitions"; Atlassian, "Motion"; Primer, "Motion and animation"). React's `<ViewTransition>` and Motion don't reduce motion unless the product asks (react.dev, "ViewTransition"; motion.dev, "MotionConfig"). WCAG's first example for 2.3.3 is a control on every page that turns animation off, and GitHub lets people set animated images to follow the system, play or stay still (Understanding 2.3.3; GitHub Docs, "Managing accessibility settings", convention).
- **Kind:** Every product: under reduced motion, parallax, movement added to scrolling, and zooming, scaling or sliding across a large part of the screen, whether people start it or it plays by itself, stop or become fades, unless the motion is essential to what it shows, such as the preview in a tool for making animations (WCAG 2.2, 2.3.3), since such motion can bring on dizziness, nausea or migraine in people with vestibular disorders, who then can't keep using the page; every change people need still shows. Current practice: smaller movement, animated blurs and small motion that plays by itself also become a fade, an instant change or a still frame; motion that follows a gesture stays; a product with a lot of motion, or with animated images people post, has its own setting for less motion, starting from the system's.
- **Applies when:** something moves.
- **Check:** in the Rendering panel, emulate `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, reload, repeat each action and scroll each page. Parallax, movement while scrolling, and zooms, slides or loops across the screen or a large part of it that still play fail; so does a press, a panel or a progress change that no longer shows at all. Small loops that still play fail current practice. Follow each link to a place on the same page, such as "Back to top", and submit a form with errors: a page that still glides to its target fails. Search the code for `prefers-reduced-motion`, and in an app that uses Motion, for `reducedMotion`. In a product with a lot of motion, or with animated images people post, look for a setting for less motion: none fails current practice, and so does one that ignores the system setting at first.
- **Fail:** a hero whose layers move at different speeds as the page scrolls, still moving under reduced motion. A product tour that zooms across the screen. A reduced-motion rule that hides the loading spinner and leaves no other sign of work. A "Back to top" link that glides up the whole page under reduced motion.
- **Pass:** under reduced motion, a drawer that appears with a 150 ms fade instead of sliding, a hero that stays still, and a progress bar that still fills.
- **Not a fail:** a drag that still follows the finger under reduced motion. A product that turns all motion off, as Atlassian and Primer do, when every change still shows.

### 4. Nothing moves, blinks or flashes on its own for long

Motion that starts by itself, or on hover, on focus or as it scrolls into view, and runs longer than five seconds beside other content, such as a carousel, a ticker, a looping animation or a background video, has a visible control to pause, stop or hide it. Stopping only while the pointer or focus stays on it is not enough. A small icon button is enough: a name that says what it does, and a target of at least 24 by 24 px. One control can stop all the motion on a page. Nothing flashes more than three times a second, unless it stays under WCAG's flash limits. Loops that only decorate stop after a few cycles, and a carousel stops while the pointer or keyboard focus is on it, and for good once people use it.

- **Why:** Continuous movement distracts people with attention deficits, so WCAG asks for a way to pause, stop or hide motion that starts by itself, lasts more than 5 s and sits beside other content (WCAG 2.2, 2.2.2); flashing more than three times a second, above set limits, can cause seizures (2.3.1). Motion that starts pulls attention (Abrams and Christ 2003), motion across the screen was rated the most distracting (Bartram et al. 2003), and flashing text raised workload and frustration (Burke et al. 2005). The W3C's carousel stops while the pointer or focus is on it (WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, "Carousel Pattern"), Baymard keeps carousels still on phones and stops them once people change slides (Scott 2025), and Primer stops small attention effects after 5 s (Primer, "Motion and animation").
- **Kind:** Every product: motion that starts by itself, or on hover, focus or scrolling into view, and lasts more than 5 s beside other content can be paused, stopped or hidden by a control or a documented keyboard shortcut, and stays paused when the pointer and focus leave, unless it is essential (WCAG 2.2, 2.2.2); nothing flashes more than three times in one second, unless all the flashing together covers no more than a quarter of any 341 × 256 px area, or each flash is faint (relative luminance changes by less than 0.1, or its darker state is at 0.8 or more) and involves no saturated red (2.3.1). Current practice: the way to pause is a visible control; decorative loops stop by themselves; carousels pause while the pointer or focus is on them, stop after people use them, and don't rotate on phones.
- **Applies when:** something moves without being asked: carousels, tickers, marquees, looping illustrations, animated backgrounds, background video.
- **Check:** load the page and wait without touching anything, then scroll each part into view, and hover and focus each card and image. For each thing still moving 5 s after it started, look for a pause, stop or hide control: none, and no documented shortcut, fails. Use the control, then move the pointer and focus away: motion that starts again fails. Count the flashes in anything that blinks: more than three in a second fails, unless it stays under the limits above. Test a video at the largest size it plays, such as full screen (Understanding 2.3.1). Hover over each carousel, and Tab into it: one that keeps rotating fails current practice.
- **Fail:** a row of customer logos that scrolls forever with no pause control. A background video with no control. A carousel that keeps rotating while someone reads a slide. A "Live" badge whose dot pulses for as long as the page is open.
- **Pass:** a carousel with a pause button that stops when hovered or focused. A looping illustration that plays three times and stops. A "Live" dot that pulses for a few seconds when data starts to arrive, then stays lit. A 32 px pause icon in the corner of a background video, named "Pause background video".
- **Not a fail:** a video that people started themselves. A loading animation that is the only thing on the page, which WCAG doesn't count as beside other content (Understanding 2.2.2).

### 5. Motion never makes people wait, and it turns around from where it is

People can act while something animates: a click during an entrance, a second press or a change of mind takes effect at once. Motion that is reversed turns around from where it is, rather than finishing first or jumping: a panel closed while it opens slides back from its current place. When people let go of something they drag or throw, such as a sheet or a drawer, it carries on at the speed of their hand rather than stopping and starting again. A jump that a click causes, such as a slider thumb moving to where people clicked its track, animates briefly. A custom drag that reaches its limit gives way a little and springs back, rather than stopping dead (default).

- **Why:** Motion that must finish first makes people wait, so Apple lets people cancel it (Apple, "Motion"). A CSS transition reversed midway turns around from where it is, in less time, while a keyframe animation can't reverse and may jump (CSS Transitions 1; CSS Animations 1), so Base UI recommends transitions and React Aria warns that keyframe animations can't be interrupted (Base UI, "Animation"; React Aria, "Styling"). A spring carries its current speed into a new direction, so motion that changes midway stays smooth (Apple, "Animate with springs"). A thumb that springs to a clicked point shows where the value went, and a drag that gives way at its end tells people they reached it without a hard stop, as Apple's scrolling views do (Interface Craft, "Creating the Ultimate Slider"; Emil Kowalski, "Apple Design").
- **Kind:** Current practice: input works during an animation; a released drag keeps its speed; state changes that can reverse use transitions or springs, not keyframe animations. Default: a jump caused by a click animates briefly; a custom drag gives way a little at its limit and springs back.
- **Applies when:** something animates in answer to people's actions.
- **Check:** open and close each menu, popover, dialog and drawer quickly, before it finishes opening, and click a control inside it during its entrance. A press that is ignored fails, and so does motion that finishes first, jumps, or restarts from the beginning. Slow the Animations panel to 10% to see where it turns around. Throw each sheet, drawer or slider people can drag, and let go mid-way: motion that stops at the release and then restarts fails.
- **Fail:** a dialog that ignores clicks until its 400 ms entrance ends. A drawer closed halfway through opening that first jumps fully open, then slides shut. A bottom sheet flicked down that stops dead at the release, then eases shut.
- **Pass:** a popover that shrinks back from wherever it was when closed early.
- **Not a fail:** an entrance that is over before people could act on it.

## Primitive

### 6. Durations fit the size of the change, and exits are quicker

Small changes on a control, such as hover, press and toggle, take about 100 to 150 ms. Menus, popovers, toasts and dialogs enter in about 150 to 300 ms, or in 150 ms or less when people open them many times a day or from the keyboard (rule 1). Large panels and changes of the whole screen take about 300 to 500 ms; a full-width sheet from the screen's edge can take 500 ms on a curve that covers most of its distance at once, and a small drawer that floats away from the edges about 200 ms (default). Leaving is quicker than entering. Motion that answers a press starts with no delay. Every duration and curve comes from a short named set, the product's own or that of the system it follows; a spring counts as one of a few named presets.

- **Why:** Long motion makes people wait (rule 1). Systems agree on about 50–150 ms for hover and press, 150–400 ms for things that enter or leave, and up to 500 ms for large areas, and give bigger changes more time (Atlassian, "Motion"; Carbon, "Motion"; Material 3, "Applying easing and duration"; Primer Primitives, motion tokens; Fluent 2, "Motion"). Longer motion helped only where people followed many moving items, as in scrolling documents, or a chart whose points move, where about a second worked best (Klein and Bederson 2005; Heer and Robertson 2007). Exits need less attention than what comes next, so Material 3 and Atlassian shorten them (Material 3, "Applying easing and duration"; Atlassian, "Applying motion"), and people judged a change faster when the old content left quickly (Huhtala et al. 2010). The rated quality of a touch button's answer drops between 100 and 150 ms of delay (Kaaresoja et al. 2014). Vaul's sheet takes 500 ms on `cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1)`, steep at the start, so it still feels quick, and shortens to 200 ms for a drawer that floats away from the edges, since the long timing reads right only on a full-width sheet (Emil Kowalski, "gestures"). Each of these systems publishes a short set of named durations and curves, as products do for spacing and corners, so motion across a product feels of one kind (Material 3, "Applying easing and duration"; Carbon, "Motion"; Fluent 2, "Motion"; Atlassian, "Motion"; Primer Primitives, motion tokens).
- **Kind:** Current practice: about 100–150 ms for changes on a control, 150–300 ms for menus, popovers, toasts and dialogs, 150 ms or less for those opened many times a day or from the keyboard, 300–500 ms for large panels and screen changes, about a second only where people follow many moving items; exits shorter than entrances; no delay before motion that answers a press; durations and curves from a short named set. Default: a full-width sheet from the screen's edge 500 ms on `cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1)`; a small floating drawer about 200 ms.
- **Applies when:** something animates.
- **Check:** read each animation's duration and delay in the Animations panel, or run `document.getAnimations().map(a => [a.effect.target, a.effect.getTiming().duration, a.effect.getTiming().delay])` while it plays. A change on a control over about 150 ms, a menu or dialog entrance over about 300 ms, or over about 150 ms for one opened many times a day or from the keyboard, a screen change over about 500 ms, an exit longer than its entrance, or a delay before motion that answers a press fails. Animations that a script drives frame by frame don't appear in that list, and Motion runs some of its springs that way: read their settings in the code. Then compare the values in use with the product's set: one outside it fails, such as a 237 ms fade among 150 and 250 ms ones.
- **Fail:** a dropdown that opens in 400 ms. A toast that leaves more slowly than it arrived. A 600 ms hover on a list row. Durations of 180, 200, 220, 237 and 250 ms on one screen.
- **Pass:** hover 120 ms; a dropdown 150 ms in and 100 ms out; a dialog 250 ms in and 200 ms out; a side panel 350 ms in.
- **Not a fail:** a chart that takes about a second to move its points to their new values. A spring with no fixed length that comes to rest within these ranges.

### 7. The curve fits the direction, linear is for steady motion, and bounce is rare

Things that enter slow down as they arrive (ease-out). Things that move or change size in place speed up, then slow down (ease-in-out). Things that leave may speed up as they go (ease-in), or reuse the entering curve, as long as the exit is short. Linear is for motion at a steady rate, such as a spinner, a progress fill or a ticker. Routine transitions don't bounce visibly: a spring may carry a slight bounce, as Apple's "snappy" spring does, as long as nothing visibly passes its resting place and comes back. A visible bounce belongs where a gesture ends, or where the product's character calls for it. When the product has no curves of its own, start from steep ones (default): `cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1)` to enter, `cubic-bezier(0.77, 0, 0.175, 1)` to move in place, and `cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1)` for a sheet.

- **Why:** Movement that starts and ends gradually was easier to follow than movement at a constant speed (Dragicevic et al. 2011). Material 3, Carbon and Atlassian decelerate what enters, ease in and out what moves in place, and accelerate what leaves for good (Material 3, "Applying easing and duration"; Carbon, "Motion"; Atlassian, "Applying motion"); some shipped components reuse one decelerating curve for exits (Radix Themes; Primer React), and no study compares the two. Linear suits only motion at a constant rate, such as rotation and spinners (Fluent 2, "Motion"; Apple, "Animate with springs"). Apple's springs have no bounce by default, and Material 3 keeps bouncy springs out of common transitions, though its expressive scheme lets components overshoot (Apple, "Animate with springs"; Material 3, "Applying transitions" and "How it works"). Apple's "snappy" spring has a bounce of 0.15 (Apple, "snappy(duration:extraBounce:)"), a damping ratio of 0.85, which passes its target by about 0.6%: on a menu, too little to see. Emil Kowalski finds the browser's own `ease-out` and `ease-in-out` too gentle to feel deliberate, and uses steeper curves that cover most of the distance early; the sheet curve is Ionic's, as Vaul uses it (Emil Kowalski, "emil-design-eng").
- **Kind:** Current practice: ease-out to enter, ease-in-out to move in place, ease-in or ease-out to leave; linear only for steady motion; no visible bounce on routine transitions. Default: `cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1)` to enter, `cubic-bezier(0.77, 0, 0.175, 1)` to move in place, `cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1)` for a sheet.
- **Applies when:** something animates.
- **Check:** read each animation's easing in the Animations panel, or from `getAnimations()` (the effect's `getComputedTiming().easing`, and each keyframe's `easing` from `effect.getKeyframes()`). An entrance with `ease-in`, movement with `linear`, or an overshoot you can see on a menu, dialog, toast or screen change fails: with the Animations panel at 10%, watch whether the element passes its resting place and comes back. A `cubic-bezier()` with a y value above 1 or below 0 overshoots, and so does a spring with bounce above 0 or a damping ratio below 1; at a bounce of 0.15 or less, the overshoot stays under 1%.
- **Fail:** a dialog that enters with `ease-in`, so it starts slowly and seems late. A menu that overshoots and wobbles each time it opens. A drawer that slides at constant speed.
- **Pass:** menus that enter with `cubic-bezier(0, 0, 0.2, 1)` and leave in 100 ms. A spinner that turns at a constant rate.
- **Not a fail:** an exit that reuses the entering curve and is short. A small overshoot at the end of a drag or a swipe. A menu on a spring with a slight bounce, such as Apple's "snappy", that never visibly passes its resting place.

### 8. Motion runs smoothly and changes only what it means to change

An animation runs without dropped frames and doesn't make the browser lay out the page again on every frame. Movement and fades use `transform` and `opacity`, which run smoothly in every browser; expanding or collapsing a section may animate its height, briefly, because what is below has to move. A transition names the properties it animates, so nothing else changes with it, and a theme switch changes every part at the same moment. Overlapping parts that fade together, such as an avatar stack, fade as one layer, so their overlaps don't show through. `will-change` is added only to fix a problem someone measured.

- **Why:** Animations that don't run on the compositor, such as moving `top` or `left`, can stutter on low-end phones and count as layout shift, while `transform` and `opacity` run on the compositor in every browser (Lighthouse, "Avoid non-composited animations"; web.dev, "Optimize Cumulative Layout Shift"; motion.dev, "Performance"; Vercel, "Web Interface Guidelines"). `transition: all` animates properties nobody meant to animate, such as every color on a theme switch (Vercel, "Web Interface Guidelines"), and overused `will-change` slows the page (MDN, "will-change"). A fast computer hides dropped frames that a mid-range phone shows, and animations that play while a page loads or fetches drop frames first (Emil Kowalski, "Animation Performance"). Parts that fade one by one show through each other where they overlap (Interface Craft, "Compositing").
- **Kind:** Current practice: no dropped frames; `transform` and `opacity` for movement and fades, and height only to expand or collapse a section; named transition properties; `will-change` only as a measured fix; a theme switch that reaches every part at once; overlapping parts faded as one layer. Default: test with the CPU slowed 4×.
- **Applies when:** something animates.
- **Check:** search the CSS for `transition: all`, `transition-property: all` and Tailwind's `transition-all`, and for transitions or keyframes on `top`, `left`, `right`, `bottom`, `width`, `margin` or `padding`, and on `height` anywhere but a section that expands or collapses. Record the main animations in the DevTools Performance panel with the CPU slowed 4× (default), or on the slowest phone the product supports, including those that play during a page change or while data loads: dropped frames during an animation fail, and so does an animation marked as not composited (a red triangle), unless it is an expanding or collapsing section. A `will-change` left on many elements at rest fails. Switch the theme: parts that fade while others change at once fail. Fade overlapping parts, such as an avatar stack, with the Animations panel at 10%: overlaps that show through fail.
- **Fail:** a sidebar that opens by animating its `width`, so the page reflows on every frame. A theme switch where the page snaps to its new colors while the buttons fade, because they carry `transition: all`. An avatar stack whose faces fade one by one, so each overlap shows through.
- **Pass:** a dropdown that scales with `transform` and fades with `opacity`, and names both in its `transition`.
- **Not a fail:** a color, background or border change on a control's hover or press, which repaints a small area. An accordion panel that animates its height as it opens, with no dropped frames.

## Component

### 9. Things come from where they belong and leave the way they came

A menu, popover or tooltip grows from its trigger; a sheet or drawer slides in from its edge; a toast enters from the edge where toasts stack; a dialog appears in place. Each leaves the way it came. Things that scale start close to full size, at 0.85 or more, never from nothing. Something that appears where it will sit, such as a dialog, a tab panel or a list item, moves only a short way as it enters, about 8 to 24 px (default); what comes from an edge still travels from that edge. Things of the same kind move the same way everywhere.

- **Why:** Motion shows where something came from and went (rule 1), so it has to match: people who reveal a view by sliding it down don't expect to dismiss it sideways (Apple, "Motion"). Atlassian opens a dropdown from its trigger and sends a dismissed panel out the way it came (Atlassian, "Applying motion"), Material 3 expands components away from the nearest screen edge (Material 3, "Transition patterns"), and Radix and Base UI set each popup's transform origin from where it sits (Radix, "Popover"; Base UI, "Popover"). Shipped systems start overlays at 0.85 to 0.98 of full size: Fluent 2 0.85, Kumo 0.9, Atlassian and shadcn/ui 0.95, coss ui 0.98. They also move what appears in place a short way, such as Fluent 2's popover by 10 px and Carbon's modal by 24 px, since a long move in a short time reads as fast and jumpy (Fluent 2, "Motion"; Carbon, "Motion"). Carbon and Atlassian give elements that do the same job the same motion, so people don't have to learn each one again (Carbon, "Choreography"; Atlassian, "Applying motion").
- **Kind:** Current practice: popups grow from their trigger, panels from their edge, exits reverse entrances, scaling starts at 0.85 or more, one motion for each kind of element. Default: what appears in place moves about 8 to 24 px.
- **Applies when:** a menu, popover, tooltip, sheet, drawer, toast or dialog animates.
- **Check:** open each of them with the Animations panel at 10%. One that grows from its own center while attached to a trigger, slides in from a side it isn't attached to, or leaves in another direction than it came fails. Read the starting scale from the keyframes or classes (such as `scale(0)` or `zoom-in-50`): under 0.85 fails. Compare two menus, or two dialogs: different motion for the same kind of element fails.
- **Fail:** a dropdown below its button that grows from its own center. A side panel that enters from the right and fades out downward. A modal that scales up from 0.
- **Pass:** a dropdown below its button that scales from 0.95 with its origin at the button, and fades out in 100 ms.
- **Not a fail:** a dialog that only fades and moves 24 px, with no scaling, as Carbon's does. A popover with no exit animation, as Fluent 2's has.

### 10. When content changes in place, the old leaves fast and the new arrives early

When content changes in place, such as a new tab panel, the next step, a filtered list or a new page, the old content goes quickly and the new content shows early; the two never sit on top of each other half-faded. An item that stays across the change, such as a card that opens into its page, moves to its new place rather than vanishing and coming back. An item people moved that lands where they may not be looking, as after a keyboard move or a drop into a list that sorts itself, is briefly marked where it lands. Items that enter or move together do so at once, or with a short stagger that ends within about half a second; in a menu people open often, nothing staggers, and a group that leaves goes all at once. An item removed from a list gives up its space at once: the items around it close the gap while it fades, not after. When steps slide, Back slides the opposite way to Next, and a step that leaves goes the way of the latest press.

- **Why:** People judged a screen change faster when the old content left quickly (Huhtala et al. 2010), and Material 3 fades old content out fully before the new fades in, and moves an element that stays into the page it opens (Material 3, "Applying transitions" and "Transition patterns"). Starting moving items at different times made them no easier to follow, and sometimes harder (Chevalier et al. 2014), and Carbon and Primer keep a whole entrance within 500 ms (Carbon, "Choreography"; Primer Primitives, motion tokens). A gap that closes only after the fade moves the rows under the pointer a moment after the press, so the next click can land on the wrong row; people closing a group have already chosen to leave, so a staggered exit only makes them wait; and a step that slides the same way for Back as for Next points to the wrong place (Emil Kowalski, "Choreographing Motion").
- **Kind:** Current practice: the old content leaves before or while the new arrives, never both half-faded; an item that stays moves to its new place; staggers end within about half a second, and menus people open often don't stagger; a group leaves at once; a removed item gives up its space at once; Back slides opposite to Next.
- **Applies when:** content changes in place, or several items enter, move or leave together.
- **Check:** switch tabs, steps, filters and pages with the Animations panel at 10%. New content that waits for an exit of the old content longer than its own entrance, old and new shown together half-faded, or an item that disappears and comes back in its new place, fails. Time items that enter together from the press until the last one is at rest (each one's delay plus its duration, from `getAnimations()`): past about 500 ms fails. A container that slides in and then staggers its contents counts as one sequence. Close a group of items, and delete a row, with the panel at 10%: a staggered exit fails, and so do neighbors that wait for the fade to end before they move. Go forward and back through each flow whose steps slide: a Back that slides the same way as Next, or a step that leaves the old way, fails. Move an item with the keyboard, and drop one into a list that sorts itself: one that lands out of the person's view with no mark fails.
- **Fail:** tabs whose old panel fades out over 300 ms before the new one starts. A filtered list that crossfades old and new rows on top of each other. Twelve cards that fade in one after another over 1.2 s. A deleted row that fades out over 300 ms, then the rows below jump up. A wizard whose steps slide left both on Next and on Back.
- **Pass:** the old panel fades out in 100 ms, then the new one fades in over 150 ms. Rows that slide to their new places at once. A deleted row that fades while the rows below slide up into its place.
- **Not a fail:** a list that changes at once, with no motion. A copy icon that turns into a check mark with a quick cross-fade on its button. No mark on a card dropped under the pointer, where it stays in view.

## Page

### 11. Scrolling stays in the person's hands

Scrolling moves the page at the speed and in the direction the person scrolls: the page never captures the wheel or a swipe to play a sequence, snap the whole page between screens, or change direction. While content streams in, such as a long answer in a chat, the page keeps the start of the new content in view instead of following its end. Movement tied to scrolling, such as parallax, stays decoration, and stops under reduced motion (rule 3). Content that animates as it scrolls into view plays once and briefly, fades text or images but not both together, and doesn't play on phones.

- **Why:** Taking over scrolling left most people at least mildly disoriented, more so on phones and with text to read (Paul, "Scrolljacking 101", 2023). Content that faded in on scroll felt slow past about 500 ms, made some people think the page had ended and worked worst on phones; the article advises playing it once, in 100–400 ms, on text or images but not both together, and not on phones (Paul, "Scroll Fading 101", 2023). Movement added to scrolling can trigger vestibular disorders, and parallax is often not essential (WCAG 2.2, Understanding 2.3.3). Chatbots that scrolled to the end of a long answer as it streamed in made people lose their place or stop reading until it finished (Kenderova et al. 2026, "AI Chatbots").
- **Kind:** Current practice: no taking over of scrolling, and no scrolling by itself to follow streaming content; reveals once, under about 400 ms, text or images but not both together, and not on phones.
- **Applies when:** the page reacts to scrolling.
- **Check:** scroll each page with a wheel, a trackpad, and touch emulation at phone width. Scrolling that stops, moves at another speed, snaps the whole page between screens or plays a sequence instead of moving the page fails. Scroll down and back up: a reveal that plays again, takes longer than about 400 ms, fades text and images in together, or plays at phone width fails. Ask for a reply longer than the screen where content streams in: a page that keeps scrolling to its last line fails.
- **Fail:** a product page that plays a 3D sequence while the wheel turns, instead of scrolling. Paragraphs that slide up and fade in over 800 ms each time they come into view.
- **Pass:** a hero image with slight parallax that stops under reduced motion, above text that scrolls normally. A screenshot that fades in once over 300 ms.
- **Not a fail:** scroll snapping inside a sideways gallery. A sticky header that shrinks as the page scrolls.

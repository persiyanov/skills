# Interaction

Covers how an interface shows what can be used and how it answers use: the signs that something can be clicked, typed in or dragged; the states of controls; the answer to a press, from the first change on screen to a long wait; disabled controls; choosing a control for a choice; keyboard, pointer and touch; where results appear; undo and confirmation; and shortcuts. Not covered here: target sizes and the space between targets, the colors and contrast of states, the danger style of buttons, how many actions show, and how things animate.

- These rules are a floor: meeting them all makes a product well made, not yet good.
- **Every product:** holds even when popular products break it, and no product's own rules change it.
- **Current practice:** what research, the design systems of top products and strong designers recommend now. A product's own documented value replaces it.
- **Default:** a starting point for designing, taken from strong designers' practice: a review finding only when a harm can be named.
- **Fail and Pass:** examples. A Pass shows one way to meet the rule, not the only one.

The rules come in four groups: rules for every level, then rules for primitives (a single control: what a press does, when it is disabled, which control a choice gets, what a field accepts), components (a widget or a form: its keys, dragging, where its results appear; a list or board whose items people change) and pages (shortcuts across a product).

## Every level

### 1. What can be used looks usable, and nothing else does

Every button, field, link and draggable item shows at rest, before any hover, that it can be used: a button by its shape or its place among actions, a field by its box, a draggable item by its handle, a link by its color, and inside a paragraph also by an underline. Nothing that can't be used borrows those signs, and every part of a control that looks usable takes the press: a checkbox's or switch's label, its box and the space between them are one target, and no layer people can't see takes the presses meant for what is under it. Whether buttons show the hand cursor is the product's choice; whichever it is, every button does the same. Controls that look alike also act alike everywhere: a click on a row opens it in every such list, or selects it in every one.

- **Why:** People find a control by the cues they can see at rest, so one without them goes unused, and the same cues on something that can't be used draw wasted presses (Norman 2008). A link marked only by hue is lost to people who can't see the difference, unless it has 3:1 contrast with the text (WCAG 2.2, 1.4.1, F73, G183); GOV.UK and Primer underline links in text, the hand cursor marks a link, a label works its control, and systems split on the cursor for buttons (GOV.UK, "Links"; Primer, "Link"; CSS Basic User Interface 3; HTML Standard, "The label element"; Tailwind CSS, "Upgrade guide"; the button styles of Primer, Carbon, Fluent 2 and GOV.UK).
- **Kind:** Every product: a link inside text differs from the text around it by more than hue, such as an underline, bold, or 3:1 contrast with the text (WCAG 2.2, 1.4.1, F73, G183); a cue that shows only on hover or focus doesn't count; no invisible layer covers a control and takes its presses. Current practice: every button, field and draggable item shows at rest that it can be used; links in body text underlined; no link color, underline, hover effect or hand cursor on anything that does nothing; one cursor for all buttons, and one behavior for controls that look alike, and never the hand on a disabled control; a checkbox's or switch's label and the space beside it work as part of the control.
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** take a screenshot with the pointer off the page. Mark what can be clicked, typed in or dragged: a control you can't pick out from the screenshot alone fails current practice, such as a button drawn as plain text in the body color. For links inside text with no underline or bold, measure the link color against the text color: under 3:1 fails; 3:1 or more with no underline fails current practice only. Move the pointer over headings, plain text, cards, images and badges: a hover effect or a hand cursor on something that does nothing when clicked fails. Compare the cursor over buttons on several screens: a mix fails. Click a row in each list and table: rows that look alike but open in one list and select in another fail current practice. Click the label, and the gap beside the box, of each checkbox, radio button and switch: one that does nothing fails current practice. Press every visible control: one that doesn't respond because a layer on top takes the press fails.
- **Fail:** Edit as plain gray text styled like the labels around it. A card that lifts and shows the hand on hover but does nothing when clicked. Words in the link color used for emphasis. A link in a paragraph marked by a blue hue at 1.3:1 against the text, underlined only on hover. A card's buttons under a transparent sheen that takes every click. A click on a row that opens the invoice in one table and only selects it in another that looks the same.
- **Pass:** links underlined inside paragraphs. Text buttons in the accent color in a toolbar, where their place shows they are actions. The arrow over every button and the hand only over links, or the hand over every button.
- **Not a fail:** navigation links and tabs with no underline: their place shows that they can be clicked, as F73 allows for navigation links.

### 2. Nothing is reachable only by hover or right-click

Hover can show a row's less-used actions, a tooltip or a preview, and right-click can open a menu, but each is a second way to reach something. Whatever appears on hover also appears when keyboard focus reaches it, and can be reached on a touch screen, which has no hover. Actions people need often stay in view. Content that appears on hover or focus stays open while the pointer moves onto it, and Escape closes it. A submenu that opens on hover stays open while the pointer moves toward it, across other items on the way. A tooltip waits a moment before it opens, so a pointer passing over it shows nothing; once one is open, the next opens at once and without animation as the pointer moves along; and keyboard focus opens a tooltip at once.

- **Why:** Touch screens have no hover and keyboards no pointer, so what only hover reveals is out of their reach, and hover content that can't be dismissed or moved onto hides the page from people using magnification (WCAG 2.2, 2.1.1, 1.4.13). People also find and use the actions in context menus less (Kaplan, "Designing Effective Contextual Menus", 2025). Tooltips that open at once flash over the content as the pointer crosses a toolbar, and a pause before every one makes scanning a toolbar slow, so Base UI and React Aria wait before the first tooltip, about 600 ms and 1.5 s, and open its neighbors at once, and both show a tooltip on keyboard focus at once (Base UI, "Tooltip"; React Aria, "Tooltip"; Emil Kowalski, "Design Engineering").
- **Kind:** Every product: content shown on hover or focus can be dismissed without moving the pointer or focus (unless it only shows an input error or covers nothing), can be hovered, and stays until the pointer or focus leaves, people dismiss it, or it is no longer true; tooltips the browser draws from `title` are exempt (WCAG 2.2, 1.4.13); the keyboard can reach whatever hover reveals (2.1.1); a touch screen, which has no hover, can reach every action and every needed piece of information that hover reveals; nothing is only in a context menu, since nothing shows that the menu exists. Current practice: what hover reveals also appears on keyboard focus, and Escape dismisses it; primary and frequent actions always in view; hover submenus that stay open while the pointer moves toward them; a pause before the first tooltip, then its neighbors at once, and a tooltip at once on keyboard focus. Default: the pause, 600 ms to 1.5 s.
- **Applies when:** something appears or changes on hover, or a context menu exists.
- **Check:** hover over rows, cards, icons, avatars and cut-off text, and note what appears. Tab to the same places: anything that appeared on hover that the keyboard can't reach, on focus or another way, fails; anything that doesn't appear on focus fails current practice. With touch emulated in a phone-sized window (DevTools device mode), or on a phone, try to reach each action and each piece of needed information: one you can't reach, in place or another way, fails. Open each tooltip and hover card, move the pointer onto it, then press Escape: one that closes as the pointer moves onto it fails; one that stays open after Escape fails, unless another key closes it, or it only shows an input error or covers nothing. Move the pointer diagonally from a menu item to its submenu, across the items below: a submenu that closes on the way fails current practice. Right-click where the product offers a context menu, and find each item somewhere visible: an item found nowhere else fails. Sweep the pointer across a toolbar: tooltips that open as the pointer merely passes fail current practice, and so does a new pause before each neighbor once one tooltip is open. Tab to a control with a tooltip: one that waits before it shows fails current practice.
- **Fail:** a row's Delete button that appears only under the mouse. A tooltip holding a link that closes as the pointer moves to the link. Rename only in a right-click menu. A submenu that closes when the pointer crosses the next item on its way there. Tooltips that flash open one after another as the pointer crosses a toolbar.
- **Pass:** row actions that appear on hover and when focus is in the row, stay visible on touch screens, and are also in the row's ⋯ menu.
- **Not a fail:** a desktop list that hides less-used row actions until hover, when they also appear on focus, can be reached on touch screens (always shown there, or shown when the row is tapped) and are in a menu.

### 3. Every control shows its state, and only while it's true

Each control has a look for each state it can be in, such as hover, pressed, keyboard focus, selected, open, disabled and busy. A state shows only while it's true: hover only under a pointer that can hover, never left behind after a tap; the pressed look while the control is pressed; the focus ring wherever keyboard focus is. Disabled controls don't react to hover or presses. A custom button can show the press by shrinking slightly, to about 0.97 of its size (default).

- **Why:** A button with no pressed look can feel as if it didn't respond (Apple, "Buttons"); Emil Kowalski scales anything pressable to 0.95–0.98 while pressed, so the press shows at once (Emil Kowalski, "emil-design-eng"), and without visible focus keyboard users lose their place (WCAG 2.2, 2.4.7). Touch screens fake mouse events after a tap, so `:hover` styles stick (React Aria, "useHover"); Material 3 and Carbon give each state a look, and disabled controls none (Material 3, "Applying states"; Carbon, "Disabled states").
- **Kind:** Every product: keyboard focus is visible on every control (WCAG 2.2, 2.4.7). Current practice: a look of its own for each state a control can be in; a pressed look on every custom button; hover only under a pointer that can hover, and never stuck after a tap; no hover or pressed look on disabled controls. Default: a custom button scales to about 0.97 while pressed.
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** for each kind of control, show each state: force hover, focus and active in DevTools (":hov"), and set the attributes for selected, open, disabled and busy. A state with no look of its own fails, and so does a custom button with no pressed look. Tab through the page: a stop with no visible focus fails. With touch emulated, or on a phone, tap a button and lift: a hover look that stays fails. Hover and press a disabled control: any change in its look fails; a not-allowed cursor doesn't count. Reload with the network slowed: a switch, checkbox or saved mark that shows a state before its real one is known, then flips, fails current practice.
- **Fail:** a custom button that looks the same at rest and when pressed. A row that stays highlighted after a tap on a phone. A focus outline removed with `outline: none` and nothing in its place. A switch that shows Off while its real state loads, then flips to On.
- **Pass:** a button that darkens while pressed, a ring on keyboard focus, and hover styles inside `@media (hover: hover)`.
- **Not a fail:** a press shown by a change of color, with no scaling. Navigation bars and other containers with no hover look, which Material 3 doesn't give them.

### 4. Every press gets an answer at once, and every wait shows it is working

Within a fifth of a second of a click, tap or key press, and ideally within a tenth, something visible changes: the pressed look, the result itself, or a busy mark on the control. When the result takes longer than about a second, the screen shows that work is going on, where the result will appear. Past about ten seconds, it shows how far along the work is, and people can keep working or cancel. Screen readers learn of the wait too. Work that has no side effects starts as soon as the need for it is known, such as an upload that starts when the file is picked, so the wait is often over before people ask; a step is never made longer to hide a wait.

- **Why:** Perception, a response people haven't prepared for and a small task have limits of about 0.1 s, 1 s and 10 s (Card et al. 1991), people notice a tap's delay from 69–96 ms (Deber et al. 2015), and Google counts 200 ms to the next frame as good (web.dev, "Interaction to Next Paint"). A spinner under 1 s only flashes and distracts, and past 10 s people need progress and a way to keep working or cancel (Primer, "Loading"; Apple, "Progress indicators"; Myers 1985). The best wait is one people never see: work started early, such as a font library sorted while people read the first steps, is done by the time they arrive (Interface Craft, "Perceived Performance").
- **Kind:** Every product: a press that starts work changes something visible within about a second, since until then people can't tell whether it worked, and press again or leave; a visible busy or progress message reaches screen readers (WCAG 2.2, 4.1.3). Current practice: the first change within 200 ms; no spinner for waits under about a second; progress and a way to keep working past about 10 s; work with no side effects started as soon as its need is known.
- **Applies when:** a click, tap or key press does something.
- **Check:** slow the network (DevTools, "Slow 4G") and the CPU (4×), then press each control that does something and watch: no visible change within about a second fails. For the main actions, read Interaction to Next Paint in the DevTools Performance panel: over 200 ms fails current practice. For a wait over about 10 s, look for progress and a way to leave or cancel. Pick a file, or reach a step that needs slow work: work that starts only at the next press, when it could have started earlier with no side effect, fails current practice. In the accessibility tree, check that the busy state or the progress is announced through a live region (`role="status"` or `aria-live`); a `progressbar` alone isn't announced when it changes (WCAG technique ARIA25).
- **Fail:** Send invite that shows nothing for three seconds and then closes the dialog. An export that shows a spinner for a minute, with no progress and no way to keep working. An upload that starts only when people press Submit, a minute after they picked the file.
- **Pass:** Send invite turns busy at once, and the dialog closes when the invite is sent. A large export that runs in the background, with a progress bar and a notice when it's done.
- **Not a fail:** a Save button that shows its pressed look at once and finishes in 300 ms with no spinner: the result is the answer. A payment or a message that waits for its button: work with side effects starts only when people choose it.

## Primitive

### 5. A press acts on release, and a second press never repeats the action

A click or tap acts when it is released over the control, so sliding off cancels it. While an action is under way, its control shows that it's busy and ignores more presses, and the server treats a repeated request as one. The busy control keeps its size, its place and keyboard focus.

- **Why:** An action on press takes away the chance to slide off and cancel a slip (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.2). People click twice from habit, on slow connections or because of tremors, so GOV.UK also asks the server to treat a repeat as one (GOV.UK, "Button"); a busy button set to native `disabled` drops focus, so Primer and React Aria keep it focusable ("Button" in both).
- **Kind:** Every product: a click or tap never acts on the press itself: it acts on release, and sliding off before release cancels it or its result can be undone, unless the release undoes what the press did, as when holding a button shows a password, or acting on press is the point, as on an on-screen keyboard (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.2); a second press while an action that sends, pays, creates or deletes is under way never repeats it, since people click twice from habit, on a slow connection or because of a tremor, and then pay, send or create twice; a busy control keeps its size, since one that grows or shrinks pushes the controls beside it and people press the wrong one. Current practice: the action runs only on release over the control; the busy control keeps keyboard focus and its place.
- **Applies when:** a control starts an action that takes time or changes something.
- **Check:** with the network slowed, click each submit, send and pay button twice quickly, then five times: a second request in the Network panel, or a duplicate result, fails. Press each button and menu item, slide off and release: an action that runs with no Undo fails; one that runs and can be undone fails current practice. While a button is busy, check that it kept its width and that focus stayed on it (`document.activeElement`), and press Enter: nothing should run again.
- **Fail:** Pay now that charges twice when double-clicked on a slow connection. A menu item that runs on `pointerdown`, so a slip can't be cancelled. A busy Save button set to `disabled`, which drops keyboard focus.
- **Pass:** Save turns busy at once, keeps its width, label and focus, ignores more presses, and the server ignores a repeat of the same request.
- **Not a fail:** actions that are safe to repeat, such as Refresh or Copy. Keys of an on-screen keyboard that act on press. A button that shows the password while held and hides it on release.

### 6. A control is disabled only when people can tell why

A submit button stays enabled: pressing it shows what's missing. A control is disabled only while it can't work right now, and the screen shows why next to it, or the reason is plain from what's on screen. A reason in a tooltip counts only when keyboard and touch can open it, which they can't on a natively disabled control. What the person can never use is hidden rather than disabled. A read-only value is not disabled: it stays readable, selectable and copyable, and so does other text people copy, such as an error code, an ID or an address.

- **Why:** A natively disabled button takes no focus, so screen-reader users moving through a form miss it, and GOV.UK, Primer and Atlassian keep submit buttons enabled and show errors instead (GOV.UK, "Button"; Primer, "Button", "Saving"; Atlassian, "Button"). Keyboard and touch can't open a tooltip on a natively disabled control (Atlassian, "Button"); Carbon hides what people lack permission for (Carbon, "Disabled states"), and a read-only field still takes focus (MDN, "HTML attribute: readonly").
- **Kind:** Every product: when a control people need is disabled, the screen shows why, or the reason is plain from what's on it, since otherwise people can't tell what to change to use it and are stuck; a reason only in a tooltip on a natively disabled control doesn't count, since keyboard and touch can't open it. Current practice: submit buttons stay enabled and check the form when pressed; a control is disabled only while its action can't run; what the person can never use is hidden; read-only values, and other text people copy, stay selectable.
- **Applies when:** a control is disabled, inactive or read-only.
- **Check:** find every disabled control: in the Elements panel, search for `disabled` and `aria-disabled="true"`. For each, find the reason on screen, or judge whether what's on screen makes it plain: neither fails. A reason only in a tooltip on a control with the `disabled` attribute fails. A submit button disabled until the form is complete fails current practice: it should stay enabled and, when pressed, show errors at the fields that need them. A read-only value that can't be selected or copied fails. An error code, ID or address people would copy that can't be selected fails current practice.
- **Fail:** Create project grayed out until every field is filled, with nothing saying which one is missing. Delete grayed out, with the reason in a tooltip only the mouse can open. An API key in a disabled field that can't be selected. An error ID that can't be selected, so people retype it for support.
- **Pass:** Create project always enabled; pressed with an empty name, it shows an error under Name and moves focus there. "Stop the running export to delete this project" under a disabled Delete button.
- **Not a fail:** Previous disabled on the first page. A dialog's confirm button disabled until the choice it confirms is made, with the choice on screen (Material 3, "Dialogs"). A busy button during its request.

### 7. The control fits the choice

Choose the control by what the choice does and how many options it has. A switch turns one setting on or off at once; a checkbox is for a choice saved with a button, or for picking several options. Radio buttons show up to about five or six options; a select holds a longer fixed list; past about ten options that people know by name, a field filters the list as they type. A segmented control switches between two to five short options that apply at once, such as views. A link takes people somewhere; a button does something. A segmented control, a button group, a toolbar or a row of tabs stays on one line: what doesn't fit scrolls or goes into a menu. Other rows of buttons, such as a form's actions, may wrap.

- **Why:** People expect a switch to act at once: 17 of 20 did even when it needed Save (Al-Jasim and Murano 2023). Systems use radio buttons for up to five or six options (Material 3, "Radio button"; Apple, "Toggles"; Fluent 2, "Radio group"; Primer, "RadioGroup"; Geist, "Radio"), a drop-down for 5 to 10 (Söderlund 2025), typing to filter past about 10 (Geist, "Select"), 2 to 5 segments (Primer, "SegmentedControl"; Apple, "Segmented controls"), and button groups, toolbars and tabs that don't wrap (Material 3, "Button groups"; Fluent 2, "Toolbar"; Kumo, "Tabs").
- **Kind:** Current practice: the control for each kind of choice, and the numbers of options above.
- **Applies when:** a screen asks people to choose, switch a view or go somewhere.
- **Check:** list each choice with what it does (applies at once, waits for a Save button, switches the view below, goes to another page), how many options it has, and its control. Each of these fails: a switch whose change waits for a Save button; radio buttons for more than about six options; a select for two to four options; more than about ten options that people know by name, with no way to type; a segmented control with more than five options; a button that goes to another page; a row of options or tabs that wraps onto a second line at any width.
- **Fail:** a switch for "Email me a weekly summary" in a form with a Save button. A select with Yes and No. Nine sort options as a segmented control that wraps onto two lines. A "View invoice" button that can't be opened in a new tab.
- **Pass:** a switch that saves at once and shows Saved. Radio buttons for three plans. A field that filters countries as people type. A segmented control for List and Board views.
- **Not a fail:** a segmented control in a form, built on radio buttons, as coss ui and Kumo build it. Seven tabs on one line that scroll sideways at narrow widths, with part of the next tab showing at the edge so people can tell there are more.

### 8. A field takes any clear form of its value

A field accepts the forms people commonly type or paste for its value: capital or small letters, spaces, hyphens and brackets, a symbol the field already shows, and the usual ways of writing it, such as "9am", "9:00" and "09:00" for a time. It ignores spaces at the ends, never blocks typing or paste, and shows what it took in its own format. It refuses only a value that can't be right or could mean two things, and says why.

- **Why:** A field that refuses a clear value makes people stop, read an error and type again, for something the product could read itself. GOV.UK accepts any format that isn't ambiguous, such as postcodes in either case and phone or card numbers with spaces or hyphens (GOV.UK, "Validation"; "Phone numbers"; "Payment card details").
- **Kind:** Current practice.
- **Applies when:** a field takes a value with a format, such as a time, a date, a phone number, a postcode, an amount or a code.
- **Check:** type three real forms of each value, with different case, spaces and separators, and paste one with spaces at the ends. A clear value refused fails, and so do keys that do nothing and blocked paste. Type a value that can be read two ways, such as "1/2/26": one taken silently in one reading fails.
- **Fail:** a time field that refuses "9am" with "Use HH:MM". A card number field that refuses spaces.
- **Pass:** "9am" shown back as 09:00. A postcode typed as "sw1a 2aa" saved as SW1A 2AA.
- **Not a fail:** 25:00 refused, with a message that says a time runs to 23:59.

## Component

### 9. Keys work the way people expect

Tab moves from one control to the next. Inside a group such as tabs, a menu, a radio group, a list or a grid, the arrow keys move and Tab leaves the group. Enter or Space presses a focused button, Enter follows a link, Space ticks a checkbox, and Enter in a text field submits its form. Escape closes the top layer, such as a menu, a popover or a dialog, and puts focus back on what opened it, and so does every other close request the platform has, such as the back gesture on Android. When a dialog opens, focus moves into it, and while a modal one is open, scrolling inside it or over its backdrop never moves the page behind; when an item is deleted or a panel closes, focus moves to the nearest sensible place, never back to the top of the page.

- **Why:** Keyboard users can use only what keys reach, and focus that gets stuck or lost stops them (WCAG 2.2, 2.1.1, 2.1.2, 2.4.3, F85). The other keys follow browsers and the W3C's patterns (HTML Standard, "Implicit submission"; MDN, "Using the Popover API"; WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, "Developing a Keyboard Interface", "Menu and Menubar Pattern", "Dialog (Modal) Pattern").
- **Kind:** Every product: everything a pointer can do works from the keyboard, without holding a key down or pressing keys within a set time, except what depends on the pointer's path (WCAG 2.2, 2.1.1); focus can leave every part with Tab, the arrow keys or Escape, or with a key the page tells people about (2.1.2); when a dialog or menu opens, focus moves into it or the next Tab reaches it, and when it closes, focus goes back to what opened it or, if that is gone, to a logical place, since otherwise keyboard users must Tab through the page to find it (2.4.3, F85). Current practice: the keys of the W3C's patterns, among them Tab staying inside an open modal dialog; after an item is deleted or a panel closes, focus moves to the nearest sensible place; the page behind an open modal stays still.
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** put the mouse away and Tab from the top of the page: a control Tab never reaches, or one that can't be used with Enter, Space or the arrows, fails, and so does one that acts only after a key is held down; so does a place focus can't leave with Tab, the arrows or Escape, unless the page says which key leaves it. With a menu open inside a dialog, press Escape once: a dialog that closes along with the menu fails current practice. Scroll inside each open modal past its end, and over its backdrop, on a phone too: a page that moves behind it fails current practice. In tabs, menus, radio groups and lists, arrows should move and Tab should leave. Open each menu, popover and dialog from the keyboard and close it with Escape: focus should land back on what opened it. Delete an item with the keyboard, and close each panel: focus left on the page itself (`document.activeElement` is `body`) fails current practice.
- **Fail:** a custom select that opens only on click. A dialog that closes and leaves focus at the top of the page. A menu that Escape doesn't close. Delete account that runs only after its button is held down for two seconds.
- **Pass:** a menu button: Enter opens it, arrows move through it, Escape closes it and focus returns to the button. Deleting a row moves focus to the next row.
- **Not a fail:** native `select` and `dialog` elements, which bring their own keys. Tab that cycles inside an open modal dialog that Escape and its buttons close. Arrow keys in a menu that wrap from the last item to the first, or stop there: the menu pattern allows both. An in-page link that jumps to a section: focus stays on the page, but the browser starts the next Tab at that section.

### 10. Whatever dragging or a gesture does, a click or tap can do

Anything done by dragging can also be done with single clicks or taps: move up and down buttons, a "Move to…" menu, a click on a slider's track. Swipes and pinches have buttons too. A drag shows what can be grabbed and where the item will land, and puts the item back if it is dropped where it can't go. A drag or gesture that is cut off leaves the control at rest and ready for the next press, and a swipe across a slider or carousel in the direction the page scrolls scrolls the page. What people drag stays under the pointer at the point where they grabbed it, with no lag; the drag goes on when the pointer leaves the item; and a quick flick counts like a long drag. Dragging an item near the edge of a list, board or panel that scrolls makes it scroll, so people can drop the item somewhere out of view. Holding a control, as in hold to record, doesn't select its label.

- **Why:** Some people can't drag, or make a gesture with several fingers or a path, and a keyboard way doesn't help those who use a pointer (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.7, 2.5.1). Atlassian shows a handle and a line where the item will land, and Apple moves an item back when a drop fails (Atlassian, "Pragmatic drag and drop"; Apple, "Drag and drop"). An item that trails the finger, jumps to its center when grabbed, or drops when the pointer slips off it feels broken, and sheets on phones close on a flick, so one that needs a long drag feels stuck (Emil Kowalski, "Apple Design" and "Design Engineering"). On phones, holding a control can select its label instead of acting (Emil Kowalski, "Feeling Native On Mobile"). Apple scrolls the place people drag over as they near its edge, and Atlassian's drag and drop scrolls a list or board by itself as an item nears its edge (Apple, "Drag and drop"; Atlassian, "Pragmatic drag and drop").
- **Kind:** Every product: whatever dragging does, single clicks or taps can do, unless dragging is essential or the browser does it (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.7), and every swipe or pinch has a tap or click way, unless the gesture itself is essential (2.5.1). Current practice: a visible handle, a mark where the item will land, and the item back in place when the drop fails; the dragged item under the pointer where it was grabbed, with no lag; the drag kept when the pointer leaves the item; a quick flick counted like a long drag; no label selected by holding a control; an area that scrolls while an item is dragged near its edge.
- **Applies when:** something can be dragged, swiped or pinched.
- **Check:** list what moves by dragging, swiping or pinching: sortable lists, boards, sliders, carousels, maps, panels that resize. For each, find a way to get the same result with clicks or taps only: none fails. Drag an item to a place it can't go: it should return. On a phone, start a drag on each slider, carousel and sortable item, then scroll, add a second finger, or switch apps: a control stuck in its dragging state fails. Swipe up and down across each slider and carousel: a page that doesn't scroll fails. Drag each custom item fast and slow: one that trails the pointer, jumps to its center when grabbed, or drops when the pointer leaves it fails current practice. Flick each sheet or drawer: one that needs a long drag to close fails current practice. On a phone, hold each control people hold: a label that gets selected fails current practice. Drag an item to the far end of a long list, and a card to a board column off screen: an area that doesn't scroll fails current practice.
- **Fail:** cards that move between board columns only by dragging. A carousel that moves only by swiping. A panel that resizes only by dragging its edge. A card that lags behind the cursor while it is dragged. A bottom sheet that ignores a quick flick and closes only after a long drag. A board whose off-screen columns a dragged card can't reach.
- **Pass:** each card's ⋯ menu has "Move to…". A carousel with previous and next buttons. A slider that jumps to where its track is clicked.
- **Not a fail:** scrolling and panning done by the browser. A signature pad, where the path is the point.

### 11. The result appears where the action happened

The result of an action shows where the person is looking: the item changes in place, a message appears next to the control or field, or the view moves to the new item. When the change itself is visible, no message is needed. A message that appears on its own, such as a toast or a status, never takes focus from what people are doing. A toast is for the result of something done in the background or out of view; it is never the only place for an error or an action, and a toast with an action stays until it is used or closed. Keyboard users can jump to the toasts with one key, and a toast's timer stops while the tab is hidden. Every result and error message reaches screen readers.

- **Why:** People need to see what their action did (Nielsen 1994), and a toast far from it can fall outside the area people magnify and reaches screen readers far from its cause, so Primer adds no message when the change shows (Primer, "Accessible notifications and messages"; "Notification messaging"). Material 3 keeps a toast with an action until it is used, and Adobe and Fluent 2 keep others up 5 to 7 s, paused on hover (Material 3, "Snackbar"; Adobe, "Toast"; Fluent 2, "Toast"). A toast sits at the end of the page's code, so its Undo is many key presses away: React Aria and Base UI reach the toasts with F6, Radix with F8 and Sonner with Alt+T (React Aria, "Toast"; Base UI, "Toast"; Radix Primitives, "Toast"; Emil Kowalski, "Working With Sonner"). A toast that expires in a hidden tab is never seen, so Sonner stops its timer there (Emil Kowalski, "Design Engineering").
- **Kind:** Every product: a message that disappears on its own holds no information or action people can't find elsewhere, unless people can turn its timer off, make it at least ten times longer, or are warned before it closes and can extend it with a simple action (WCAG 2.2, 2.2.1), since people who read slowly or look away lose it; result and error messages that appear without taking focus reach screen readers (4.1.3). Current practice: results in place or next to what caused them; messages that appear on their own never take focus; no message when the change shows; errors and actions never only in a toast; toasts that disappear stay at least 5 s and pause while hovered or focused, or while the tab is hidden; one key that takes the keyboard to the toasts.
- **Applies when:** an action has a result people need to know: saved, sent, failed, or done in the background.
- **Check:** do each main action and note where its result appears: a result shown only far from the action, when it could show in place, fails current practice. Let each toast expire: one that held an action, such as Undo, Retry or View, or an error, that can't be found anywhere else fails; one whose action can also be found elsewhere fails current practice. In the accessibility tree, check that each result and error message that appears without taking focus is in a live region (`role="status"`, `role="alert"`, `role="log"` or `aria-live`): one that isn't fails. Note where focus is before and after each message appears: focus moved to a toast or status fails current practice. With the keyboard only, reach a toast's action: no key that jumps to the toasts fails current practice. Start an action that ends in a toast, switch to another tab, and come back: a toast that expired while hidden fails current practice.
- **Fail:** a Save error shown only in a toast that closes after 4 s. "Deleted. Undo" in a toast that closes after 5 s, with no other way to restore the item. "Saved" in a corner toast while the edited field shows nothing. A "Saved" toast that takes focus away from the form.
- **Pass:** "Saved" next to the Save button. An error under the field that caused it. "Export ready" with a View link, in a toast that stays until closed, and the export also listed on the Exports page.
- **Not a fail:** a switch that turns on with no message: the switch shows the result. A disappearing toast that repeats what the page already shows. A dialog that needs an answer, such as a warning that the session is about to end, taking focus.

### 12. Small changes show at once, and a failed one visibly goes back

Small, frequent changes that almost always succeed, such as renaming, starring, reordering or moving a card, show their result at once, before the server confirms. If the server rejects one, the item returns to its true state where the person can see it, with a message saying what failed. Changes that wait for a connection show that they are waiting. Payments, sending, and anything that can't be undone wait for the server: people act on a result they see, and putting the screen back can't undo what they did next.

- **Why:** A change made on the device can show within one frame, well under the 69–96 ms at which people notice a tap's delay (rule 4). On failure, React and Vercel put back the confirmed value and tell the person (React, "useOptimistic"; Vercel, "Web Interface Guidelines"), and Linear counts the changes waiting to sync (Linear, "Download Linear", convention).
- **Kind:** Every product: a change shown before the server confirmed it never looks saved when it isn't: if the server rejects it, the item returns to its true state and the screen says what failed, and while it is held for a lost connection, it shows that it's waiting. Otherwise people act on, or leave, something false. Current practice: optimistic updates for small changes that almost always succeed; payments, sending and anything that can't be undone wait for the server.
- **Applies when:** the product shows a change before the server confirms it.
- **Check:** in the Network panel, block the requests (or go offline), then make each small change: a change that stays as if saved, with no message and no sign that it's waiting, fails. Reload: the screen should show the server's state.
- **Fail:** a renamed file keeps its new name after the request failed, and the old name comes back on reload.
- **Pass:** a card moves to Done at once; when the server rejects the move, the card goes back to In progress with "Couldn't move: you no longer have access" and Retry.
- **Not a fail:** changes made offline, kept with a visible count of waiting changes and sent when the connection returns. A payment that waits for the server behind a busy button.

### 13. Undo what can be undone; confirm only what can't

Common actions that can be reversed, such as archiving, removing from a list or moving to trash, happen at once and offer Undo. A confirmation is kept for what can't be undone or costs a lot when done by mistake: deleting for good, sending, paying, removing someone's access. It names what will happen to what, and the safe choice has focus. Typing a name to confirm is kept for the rare, widest losses, such as deleting a workspace. Closing a panel or leaving a page with unsaved changes keeps them, or asks first. In an editor or a board, Cmd or Ctrl+Z undoes the person's last actions one at a time and shows what came back.

- **Why:** People stop reading a warning they see often: after three weeks they followed an unchanging security warning 55% of the time, down from 87% (Vance et al. 2018), and the same happens to confirmations shown too often (Nielsen 2018). Typing the key detail kept attention but cost time (Bravo-Lillo et al. 2013), so Primer keeps it for the widest damage, names what is affected and skips confirmation for what can be undone (Primer, "Delete"); the W3C puts focus on the least destructive choice, and Apple asks before closing loses work (WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, "Dialog (Modal) Pattern"; Apple, "Modality").
- **Kind:** Every product: an action that changes or deletes people's saved data, commits them legally, moves their money or submits their answers to a test can be undone, has its input checked for errors with a chance to correct them, or can be reviewed, confirmed and corrected before it is final (WCAG 2.2, 3.3.4), since otherwise one slip loses data or money people can't get back. Current practice: no confirmation for common actions that can be undone; step-by-step undo in editors and boards, showing what came back; a confirmation that names the item and the result, with focus on the safe choice; typing a name only for the widest losses; closing or leaving with unsaved changes keeps them or asks first.
- **Applies when:** an action deletes, sends, pays, removes access or submits test answers, a confirmation exists, or a panel or page holds changes that aren't saved yet.
- **Check:** list each action that deletes, sends, pays, removes access or submits test answers, and how it is protected: undo, a check of what people entered with a chance to correct it, a confirmation, or nothing. One that deletes the person's data, pays, commits them or submits test answers with none of these fails. A confirmation for a common action that can be undone fails current practice. In each confirmation, check that it names the item and the result, and which button has focus. Check that each Undo stays reachable as rule 11 asks. Edit a form, then leave it each way: Escape, a click outside, the close button and the browser's Back. Losing the edits without a word fails current practice; Cancel may discard them. In an editor or board, make three changes, then press Cmd or Ctrl+Z three times outside a text field: a press that does nothing, stops after one step, or changes something off screen without showing it, fails current practice.
- **Fail:** Delete project acts at once, with no undo and no confirmation. "Are you sure?" before archiving an email that can be restored. Typing the name to remove one tag. A settings panel that closes on a click outside and loses its edits. A board where Cmd+Z after moving three cards does nothing.
- **Pass:** Archive acts at once, offers Undo, and the item stays in Archive. "Delete 3 projects and their 240 issues? This can't be undone." with Delete projects and Cancel, and focus on Cancel. Typing the workspace name to delete a workspace.
- **Not a fail:** a confirmation before sending an email to 5,000 people, which can't be recalled.

### 14. Shortcuts are easy to find and hard to press by accident

A product people use every day gives its frequent commands keyboard shortcuts, shows each next to its command (in the menu item, the tooltip, the command palette), and lists them all in one place, often under ?. A shortcut never takes a key the browser or the system uses for something else. A shortcut that is a single letter, number or symbol can be turned off, or works only while its part of the page has focus.

- **Why:** A single-key shortcut fires when people mistype, or when people controlling the computer by voice speak a word (WCAG 2.2, 2.1.4). People rarely learn shortcuts shown only in menus, but used them for 94–99% of selections when all showed while the modifier was held (Grossman et al. 2007; Malacria et al. 2013), and GitHub and Linear list them under ? (GitHub, "Keyboard shortcuts"; Linear, "Navigating Linear"; convention). Apple keeps shortcuts for frequent commands, and a shortcut on a key the browser, the system or assistive technology uses breaks that key (Apple, "Keyboards"; WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, "Developing a Keyboard Interface").
- **Kind:** Every product: single-character shortcuts can be turned off, given a modifier, or work only on focus (WCAG 2.2, 2.1.4). Current practice: shortcuts for frequent commands, shown next to each command and listed in one place; none takes a browser or system key for another job.
- **Applies when:** the product has keyboard shortcuts, or people use it every day.
- **Check:** open the shortcut list (try ? and Cmd or Ctrl+K) and the menus. For each single-key shortcut, look for a setting that turns it off or adds a modifier key to it, or check that it works only when its area has focus: a shortcut with none of these fails. Press the browser's own keys (Cmd or Ctrl with L, R, D, F): one that does something else in the product fails current practice.
- **Fail:** pressing D in a list deletes the selected row, and nothing turns single-key shortcuts off. Cmd+D duplicates a row instead of bookmarking the page. Shortcuts listed only in a help article.
- **Pass:** ? opens the shortcut list, menu items show their shortcuts, and a setting turns single-key shortcuts off.
- **Not a fail:** a marketing site with no shortcuts. Ctrl+F opening the product's own search over a long list that the browser's find can't search, which keeps the key's job. Cmd or Ctrl+K opening a command menu, the common key in work tools, where people can change it.

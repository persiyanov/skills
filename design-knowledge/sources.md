# Sources

Each source cited in these skills, once, with what we use from it and where to find it.

## Adding a rule or a source

- An idea taken from a source names it in parentheses, briefly: author and year for research and writing, organization and page for standards and design systems, and a short title only where one author has two works in a year. This file lists each source once, with its full title, what we use from it and where to find it. A citation that opens a section covers the list under it. A claim without a source follows from logic stated in its line, such as geometry, arithmetic or what a spec defines.
- A rule's Why says what fails for people, how, and the limit that sets the rule, in a line or two. Study details and surveys of what each system does stay in this file.
- A part of a rule holds in **every product** when breaking it makes people fail: they can't see, read, reach or use what they need, or they do the wrong thing, such as press the wrong button or miss a warning. Every WCAG 2.2 AA criterion is such a part. The rule says how the failure happens and keeps the source's own exceptions. A style popular products use still fails it, and the rule shows the smallest change that passes. Every other part is **current practice**: what research, the design systems of top products and strong designers recommend now.
- A rule goes in when it holds against a real counter-case, isn't obvious, and leaving it out makes a product work or feel clearly worse. How a product feels counts as much as whether people fail: for current practice, it is enough that strong designers or design systems do it, with no study needed.
- A rule says what people need, not one way to build it, and covers only the cases its evidence covers. Its Pass shows one way to meet it.
- Each file stands alone: it never links to another file, and a rule links only to earlier rules in it. What a rule needs from another subject, it states in place, with its source.
- A number a product can fail needs research, a standard, or the first principle behind it, stated in the line. A number that rests only on one system's or one designer's choice goes in marked "default": a starting point for designing, and a finding in review only when a harm can be named.
- Every source is current in 2026:
  - standards: the version in force, such as WCAG 2.2 and the current CSS and HTML specs;
  - design systems and products: as published now, never a replaced or archived version, such as Material 2 or Polaris React;
  - studies of how people use a kind of interface, surveys of what products do, and writing by designers and other practitioners: from the last three years (in 2026, from 2023 on);
  - research on how people see, read, move, remember and decide: any age, unless later work overturned it. The test: a finding that would change as people get used to new conventions dates, so it belongs in the line above.

  An older guideline that still holds is stated by a current source: cite that one. A claim left with no current source is removed, not reversed: its opposite needs evidence of its own. Check the sources against this once a year.
- A product's help pages show what products commonly do, not design guidance, so they are cited as "convention".
- Skills written by working product designers count as practitioners' writing: Interface Craft (Josh Puckett), Emil Kowalski's skills and the interfaces plugin (Jakub Krehel). Other agent skills are cited only for a value marked "default", or for an idea no older source was found for.
- The files state ideas in our own words, never a source's text.
- Sources that can't be fetched again (paid, behind a login, or likely to change) keep a local copy in `library/`, which git ignores, so these copies are never published.

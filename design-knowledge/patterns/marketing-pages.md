# Marketing pages

Covers pages that explain and sell a product to people who don't use it yet: home and landing pages, feature pages, pricing pages, and the explainer steps of a sign-up flow. It covers the positioning these pages follow, what they must make clear, the order and length of what they say, the main action, pictures of the product, proof, prices, the doubts that stop a purchase, the forms on them, and where persuasion stops. Not covered here: spacing, what stands out, type, color, surfaces, icons, interaction and motion in general, and the content and words of the product itself.

A marketing page may persuade. Favorable framing of true facts and exaggeration nobody takes literally are fine; false facts and tricks are not. The rules come in three groups: rules for every level, then rules for the page (the first screen, the main action, the order and length) and for its components (pictures, proof, prices, doubts, forms).

## Every level

### 1. The page follows the positioning the company chose

Positioning is the company's choice of who the product is for, what those people would use instead, what the product does that those alternatives don't, what that gets them, and what kind of product it is. The page follows it: the first screen leads with what sets the product apart for its customers, and the headings, read in order, make the case for it. A core feature that isn't part of that difference may have a section further down, but not the headline. The positioning comes from the company's own sources, such as a positioning or messaging document, a sales deck or the owner, and is never guessed from the code or from the feature that is easiest to show.

- **Why:** Positioning says why a group of customers should pick the product over what they would use instead; its five parts are those alternatives, what the product does that they don't, the value that brings, the customers who care most about it, and the kind of product it is, and marketing and sales build on it (Dunford 2026, "Obviously Awesome"). A company with several products first decides whether it positions a product, a suite or the company; before launch a positioning is only a thesis, kept loose; and positioning for investors looks further ahead than for customers (Dunford 2026, "Positioning Prep Part 1"). A homepage shows how the product differs in what it brings people, not in features (Wang 2024), and the competitors that count are those on the buyers' short list (Dunford 2026, "Positioning in the Age of AI"). Teams that struggle mistake messaging for positioning or position a feature instead of its value, and positioning one team works out alone fails in use (Business of Software 2026). Which alternatives buyers weigh and which customers care most are facts about buyers that neither the code nor the page reliably holds, so a positioning guessed from them is built from the features they show.
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** before judging anything else, find the positioning in the company's own sources: a positioning or messaging document, a sales deck, or the owner; before launch, the team's current best guess counts. A deck or memo for investors doesn't count: it positions the company for investors, who look further ahead. On a site for several products, use the positioning of what the page sells: the company's on the home page, the product's on its own page. Write down its five parts: a part the sources don't give is a question for the owner, not a guess. When sources disagree, use the newest and name the others in the finding. Then read the first screen and the headings alone, in order: if they put something else first, such as a feature outside the difference, another audience or an alternative the buyers don't consider, they fail, and the finding names the source and its date, so the owner can say whether the page follows a newer choice. With no source at all, write down the positioning the page implies and mark it unconfirmed: a redesign may say it more clearly and more specifically, but leading with a different feature, audience or alternative is a question for the owner.
- **Fail:**
  - a project tool positioned for agencies that plan client work, with a headline about its time tracker because the tracker is easy to show;
  - a budgeting app that compares itself with a big bank's app its buyers don't consider, when its positioning says they track their money in a spreadsheet today;
  - a redesign that moves another feature into the headline with no positioning source behind it.
- **Pass:**
  - for an invoicing tool positioned for freelancers who invoice from spreadsheets today, "Invoicing for freelancers. Send an invoice in a minute and get paid by card", with automatic reminders, a core feature, in their own section further down;
  - a feature page, or a campaign page for one group of buyers, that leads with its own feature or group without contradicting the positioning;
  - a pricing page that leads with its plans;
  - a home page that leads with a new launch, for a company most visitors already know;
  - wording that differs from the positioning document's: the positioning says what to get across, and the page chooses the words.

### 2. Every claim is true, and nothing is faked

Exaggeration nobody takes literally, such as "the fastest way to ship", and favorable framing of true facts, such as "100+ teams" for 104, are fine. What a buyer would take as fact is true and can be backed by the company's data: figures, comparisons, named customers, results, ratings, certifications, "most popular", "only this week", "no card needed". A testimonial comes from a real customer who used the product, in their words, with any payment or tie disclosed. A customer logo belongs to a real customer and doesn't suggest an endorsement that doesn't exist. Pictures show the product as it is.

- **Why:** People choose and pay on what they take as fact. In the US, fake reviews and testimonials are banned, and so are hiding negative reviews while showing the rest as all of them and passing off a company-run review site as independent (FTC, 16 CFR Part 465); an endorser shown as a user must have used the product, a result shown as typical must be backed, and a company's name or logo can be an endorsement (FTC, Endorsement Guides). Exaggeration ordinary buyers don't take seriously is left alone, but a claim people reasonably read as fact must be true (FTC, "Policy Statement on Deception"), even inside a testimonial or quotation marks (ASA, "Puffery and expressions of opinion"). For consumers in the EU and the UK, fake reviews, false endorsements, trust marks shown without authorisation and false "limited time" claims are banned outright (Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, Annex I; DMCCA 2024, Schedule 20), and in 2026 a UK court confirmed that fake countdown timers and false "high demand" messages broke the law (CMA, "Emma Sleep", 2026).
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** list every claim a buyer could take as fact, including those inside testimonials, badges and labels such as "most popular". For each, look for its source in the company's data, docs or contracts: a claim shown to be false fails; one you can't trace is a question for the owner, not a fail. Note any countdown's end time, reload the page in a new private window, and open it again after that time: a countdown that restarts, or the same offer still running after it ends, fails. Compare each product picture with the product: a screen or feature it doesn't have fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "Trusted by 10,000 teams" for a product with 300 customers;
  - a countdown that restarts on every visit;
  - a quote from "Sarah, Marketing Lead" whom no one can find;
  - a SOC 2 badge without a SOC 2 report.
- **Pass:**
  - "Trusted by 300+ teams";
  - a quote from a named customer, with their role and company, given with their permission;
  - "The best way to build software";
  - "100+ customers" for 104;
  - a figure with no footnote or source line, when the company can back it;
  - a simplified picture of the real product with no "illustration" label.

### 3. Claims are specific, in the buyer's words

Claims say what the product does and what that gets people, with a concrete detail: a number, a named result, a scene from real use, rather than an adjective. The words are the ones buyers use for their problem; terms of the trade appear when the buyers are in the trade. A line that any product could use, such as "Supercharge your workflow", "The future of work" or "Powered by AI", says nothing about this one.

- **Why:** A line any product could use tells buyers nothing about this one. Objective claims persuaded more than subjective ones (Darley and Smith 1993), specific green claims seemed more credible than general ones (Ganz and Grimes 2018), and descriptions with relevant concrete details were trusted more (Trzebiński et al. 2023). Newcomers weigh benefit messages closely and experts attribute messages (Maheswaran and Sternthal 1990), so a page states the outcome and backs it with how. Needlessly complex words made writers seem less intelligent (Oppenheimer 2006), and naming "AI" in a product description lowered intention to buy, through lower trust (Çiçek et al. 2024): AI is a technology, not a benefit (Samsonov 2025).
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** for each headline and claim, ask whether a competitor could use it unchanged: one that could fails, unless a concrete detail sits next to it. Read each headline with its subhead: if together they don't say what the product does or what it gets people, they fail. Search the text for words that fit any product, such as "seamless", "unlock", "supercharge", "leverage", "next-generation", "revolutionize" and "AI-powered": each one with no concrete claim beside it fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "Supercharge your workflow with AI-powered insights.";
  - "The future of collaboration."
- **Pass:**
  - "Send invoices in a minute and get paid by card.";
  - "Close the month in 3 days instead of 10", when customers' data shows it;
  - a short brand or outcome headline, such as "Build in a weekend", over a subhead that says what the product is;
  - a trade term the buyers use themselves, such as "SOC 2" for security teams.

## Page

### 4. The first screen says what the product is and what to do next

Within the first screen, at every width, a page for a product people may not know says what the product is or does and offers the main next step, and the real product, or what it makes, shows in it or right below it. Who it is for is clear from the first screen or the one after. A headline may lead with the outcome, as long as the subhead or the picture says what the product is. The subhead is prose, one to three short sentences; further claims go to the sections below. One huge headline or image doesn't fill the whole first screen.

- **Why:** The first screen decides whether people scroll, so it says in a short tagline what the company does and makes the next step clear (Wang 2024); people testing software sites looked for pictures of the interface, and pictures near the top of the homepage drew them in (Collins 2025). People see about half of a page on average, on desktop and on phones (Contentsquare 2024), so the first screen is what most visitors certainly see, and on desktop, wide gaps, oversized type and large images make people scroll more and understand less (Flaherty et al. 2023). Of 11 top product homepages, 10 named what the product is in the headline or subhead and 5 named its audience there, and no subhead on them or their feature pages is a list: each is one to three short sentences (11-site survey, 2026, convention).
- **Applies when:** a page introduces the product to people who may not know it.
- **Check:** look at the first screen at 1440 and 390 px wide for five seconds, then write down what the product is, what it does for people and what to do next: anything you can't write fails. Find the main action at both widths without scrolling: missing fails. No picture of the real product, or of what it makes, in the first screen or right below it fails. Read the subhead: one set as a list, or longer than three sentences, fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "The future of work" over an abstract gradient, with "Get started";
  - a first screen at 390 px filled by the headline, with the action below it;
  - a subhead split into a lead line and three feature bullets.
- **Pass:**
  - "Invoicing for freelancers. Send an invoice in a minute and get paid by card", beside a screenshot of an invoice, with "Start for free";
  - an outcome headline over a subhead that says what the product is;
  - the audience named in the second screen.

### 5. One main action, in plain words, wherever people decide

A page has one main action, styled to outrank the others; a second action for a different buyer, such as "Contact sales" next to "Start for free", is fine. The main action shows in the first screen and again where people finish reading, at the end of the page. Its label says what happens next or what people get: "Start for free", "Book a demo", and "Start free trial" only when a trial starts. A way to decline is plain and doesn't shame.

- **Why:** A generic label doesn't tell people what they will get (Wang 2024), and competing calls to action weaken each other (Laubheimer 2023, "3 Common IA Mistakes"). Of 11 top product homepages, 6 paired a self-serve action with a sales action, and 8 offered the main action again at the end (11-site survey, 2026, convention). Shaming people who decline pushes them toward the choice the business wants, a deceptive design (Rosala 2023; FTC, "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light").
- **Applies when:** always.
- **Check:** count the actions in the main style in each screen: two or more that compete for one buyer fail. The same action repeated further down counts once. Read each action's label with the text beside it: one that doesn't say what happens next fails, and so does "Start free trial" where no trial starts. Scroll to the end: no main action there fails. Read every way to decline: one that mocks or blames the reader fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "Get started", "Learn more" and "Book a demo" as three filled buttons in the first screen;
  - "Start free trial" that opens a form for a paid annual plan;
  - "No thanks, I prefer losing customers."
- **Pass:**
  - "Start for free" as the main button and "Contact sales" beside it in a quieter style, both repeated at the end of the page;
  - "Get started" when the text beside it says what follows, such as "Free for up to 3 users";
  - the same main action offered again further down;
  - a "Sign up" link in the header.

### 6. The page answers buyers' questions, at the length they need

After the first screen, the page answers what buyers ask next: how it works, what it does for someone like them, why believe it, what it costs or where to find out, and what happens if it doesn't work out. Each section makes one point under its own heading, with a picture where it helps. The page is as long as a first-time buyer needs: a simple, cheap product gets a short page; a product a team buys gets the sections its buyers ask for, each one earning its place. Site pages keep the site's navigation; a sign-up step may drop it.

- **Why:** Buyers of business software need detailed, open content before they feel sure enough to try or buy, and hesitate or leave without it (Collins 2025). When people care about a decision, the quality of each argument decides; when they don't, they may count arguments instead (Petty and Cacioppo 1984), so for buyers who care, a section helps only when it is strong. Across 41,000 landing pages from one page builder, shorter, plainer copy went with higher conversion, a correlation rather than a test (Unbounce 2024). Top product homepages run from the first screen through proof and four to eight feature sections, each with its own picture, to a last call to action, and all 7 of their feature pages checked keep full navigation (11-site survey, 2026, convention); Baymard removes the site's navigation from checkout, a single task, and keeps the logo as a way out (MacDonald 2026, "The Checkout UX Guide").
- **Applies when:** a page runs past its first screen.
- **Check:** list the sections, and write the buyer question each one answers: a section that answers none, or repeats another, fails. Read the headings alone, in order: if they make no case at all, they fail; whether it is the right case is rule 1. A row of cards with an icon, a two-word heading and a vague line each, standing in for how the product works, fails.
- **Fail:**
  - three cards headed "Fast", "Secure" and "Simple" as the only explanation of the product;
  - a feature page whose sections repeat the homepage's claims with no new detail.
- **Pass:**
  - headings that read as the case: "Send invoices in a minute", "Get paid by card or bank", "Reminders go out for you", "Used by 3,000 freelancers", "Free for 3 clients, then $9 a month";
  - a long page for a product a team buys, when every section answers a question.

## Component

### 7. Each picture of the product makes the point its text makes

Pictures show the real product: screenshots, crops of them, or simplified drawings of real screens, near the top and beside the sections they explain. Each picture shows the one thing its section says, with only the parts needed to show it: the rest of the screen, details that don't serve the point, and decoration that competes with it are left out. Motion is a short, silent loop of the product in use that people can pause, and that stays paused until they start it again, or a video they start themselves, and the page makes its case without it. A small icon button on the picture is enough to pause it: a name that says what it does, and a target of 24 by 24 px.

- **Why:** People testing software sites looked for pictures of the interface, and real screenshots worked better than graphical stand-ins; animated GIFs suited features that move, and video came only after enough images (Collins 2025). Interesting but irrelevant detail lowers what people learn: leaving it out is among the largest effects in Mayer's design principles (Cromley and Chen 2025), and it lowered learning across 58 studies (Sundararajan and Adesope 2020), though only a little in the newest and largest meta-analysis, of 50 studies with about 20,700 students (Cheng et al. 2026, "Seductive Details"). These studies measured learning, not purchases: a picture is clearer with less, not proven to sell more. In a software tutorial, simplified drawings of an interface drew the eye to the right place but didn't help people do the task better than plain screenshots (Bsdurrek and Meng 2024), so simplifying removes clutter but doesn't replace the real thing. Motion and sound that start on their own pull people away from what they read, so WCAG requires a way to pause, stop or hide motion that lasts more than five seconds beside other content, unless it is essential, and a way to pause or stop sound that lasts more than three seconds, or set its volume apart from the system's (WCAG 2.2, 2.2.2 and 1.4.2). Motion that stops only while focus stays on it isn't paused, since it ties people up (Understanding 2.2.2), and the same holds for the pointer; a 24 by 24 CSS px target is enough for the control (2.5.8).
- **Applies when:** a page shows pictures, animations or video of the product.
- **Check:** for each picture, write the point of its section, then point at the parts of the picture that show it: panels, rows, labels, figures or decoration that show nothing about it fail. Compare each picture with the real product (rule 2). Load the page and wait, then scroll each picture into view and wait again: sound that starts by itself and runs past three seconds with no control to pause, stop or turn it down fails, and so does motion beside other content that runs past five seconds with no control to pause, stop or hide it. Use each pause control, then move the pointer and focus away: motion that starts again fails. A video that starts by itself fails.
- **Fail:**
  - a full dashboard with twenty widgets beside a heading about one export button;
  - a card about approving drafts crowded with timestamps, tags and counters;
  - a hero video that starts with sound.
- **Pass:**
  - the real export dialog, cropped, beside "Export to Excel in one click";
  - a card with one drafted message and an "Approve" button beside "Nothing goes out until you approve it";
  - a 24 px pause icon in a corner of a looping product animation, named "Pause animation";
  - a simplified drawing of a real screen, with no "illustration" label;
  - a section whose point is a price or a policy, with no picture;
  - a silent loop of a few seconds that plays once.

### 8. Proof says who and what changed, and can be checked

Proof shows who says it and what changed: a quote with the person's full name and, for business buyers, their role and company; a customer story with one specific result; logos of customers buyers know, used with the customers' agreement and in the colors they allow; ratings that link to the review site they come from. Figures that show popularity are large and real, or show growth; a small count, or a message that few people use the product yet, works against it.

- **Why:** On tour-booking sites, some people ignored a company's own glowing testimonials but trusted independent reviews with some negatives, and rating badges that couldn't be clicked looked broken (Scott 2023, "Always Link to Third-Party Sources"); 77% of business software buyers read user reviews before buying (TrustRadius 2025). Showing what most people choose raised demand for it by 13 to 20% in a field experiment (Cai et al. 2009), but a message that a behavior is common makes it more common, even an unwanted one (Cialdini et al. 2006), so "join 12 teams" tells buyers few chose this, though no study has tested small counts on product pages. When a behavior is still rare, saying it is growing moved people more than its current share (Sparkman and Walton 2017). Top product sites show named quotes with role and company, logos of well-known customers, one result per customer story, and large share figures such as "half of the Fortune 500" (11-site survey, 2026, convention).
- **Applies when:** a page shows testimonials, logos, ratings, customer stories or popularity figures.
- **Check:** for each quote, look for the full name and, on a page for business buyers, the role and company: a first name or initials only fails. Click each rating, award and review badge: one that links to nothing, or not to its source, fails. Read each popularity figure: a small count, or one that says few people use the product, fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "Great product! — Sarah K.";
  - "4.9 ★" with no link;
  - "Join 37 teams already using it."
- **Pass:**
  - "We closed the books in 3 days instead of 10. — Dana Ruiz, CFO, Northwind";
  - "4.6 on G2", linking to the product's G2 page;
  - a row of customer logos with no quotes, or in one color, where the customers agreed to that use.

### 9. Prices are findable, complete and easy to compare

Where people can buy a plan by themselves, its price is on the site, one step from any page, with no sign-up or call needed. A price is optional where buying goes through sales, with a demo or a quote, and where there is nothing to buy yet, or the price is still being worked out: a waitlist, a private beta, a company in stealth, an early product. Wherever people pay, the full price shows before they pay. A price shown is what people will pay each billing period, with every mandatory fee; a per-month figure for a yearly plan stands next to the amount charged. Plans sit side by side, about five at most, with every feature listed as a row under group headings, explained where it appears, and none hidden behind "View more". A plan marked "most popular" is the most popular.

- **Why:** Transparent pricing is the first thing business software buyers would change about buying it (TrustRadius 2025). Every top product site checked sells plans by itself, publishes their prices, links to them from its home page and keeps "Contact sales" for its enterprise plan (11-site survey, 2026, convention), while only 4% of software profiles on G2, about 9,300, list a price (Paul 2025). A quarter of software sites hid plan features behind "View more", and some people then assumed the features were missing; unexplained terms and features with no link to their page slowed people down (Collins 2025). People weigh options against each other only among a few, usually under five to seven (Moran and Dykes 2024). People decide on the price they see: for consumers, a price shown with a way to buy includes taxes and every mandatory fee (Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, Article 7(4)(c); DMCCA 2024, section 230), and a subscription's price is the total for each billing period (Consumer Rights Directive, Article 6(1)(e); CMA, "Providing clear and accurate information about prices"). The UK's first fine under that law, £4.2 million in 2026, was for a mandatory £3 booking fee left out of the price shown (CMA, "AA and BSM driving schools", 2026), and on a ticket site, buyers who saw fees only at checkout spent about 21% more (Blake et al. 2021). A monthly figure for yearly plans raised revenue in one company's test and caused accidental yearly purchases at another, until the amount charged today was shown (Mazur 2023); all 6 top sites with a billing toggle open it on yearly (11-site survey, 2026, convention).
- **Applies when:** people can buy a plan through the site, or the site shows prices.
- **Check:** where people can buy a plan by themselves, reach the prices in one step from the home page, a feature page and the footer: if you can't, ask the owner whether the price is still being worked out. Compare the first price shown with the total on the last step before paying: a difference other than optional extras, usage charges the page explains, or taxes the law lets the seller add later (such as US sales tax, or VAT for business buyers) fails. Switch any billing toggle: a per-month figure without the amount charged beside it fails. Look for "View more", collapsed rows or tooltips holding plan limits: each fails. Ask the owner for the data behind any "most popular" label.
- **Fail:**
  - "$8 per month", with "billed yearly" and the $96 total only in a tooltip;
  - plan limits behind "See all features";
  - "Most popular" on a plan few people choose.
- **Pass:**
  - "$8 per month, billed yearly ($96)", beside "$10 billed monthly";
  - no public prices on a site that sells through demos or quotes, or on a waitlist, a private beta, a company in stealth or an early product;
  - "Contact sales" for an enterprise plan with negotiated terms;
  - a billing toggle that opens on yearly, with the amount charged in view.

### 10. Doubts are answered where people decide

The page answers the doubts that stop a purchase, where they come up: what happens after a trial, whether a card is needed, how to cancel, whether the data is safe, whether it works with the buyer's tools. A free plan, a trial, monthly billing, a refund policy or "no card needed" sits near the main action when it is true and answers a doubt this offer raises: "no card needed" and "cancel anytime" belong where people will pay, or would expect to give a card. Security and compliance appear as plain claims with a link, such as "SOC 2 Type 2" linking to a trust center. A trial or plan that renews says what it costs, when it renews and how to cancel before card details are taken.

- **Why:** Buyers check that a product fits their tools and their kind of business, and assume it doesn't when they can't tell (Collins 2025). Without the terms first, people pay for what they didn't choose: in the US, a sale that renews or turns paid must disclose all material terms before taking billing details, get express consent and offer a simple way to stop (ROSCA, whose reach to business buyers is unsettled); for consumers, California requires express consent to the renewal terms and, for sign-ups made online, a way to cancel online, for contracts made since 1 July 2025 (California Business and Professions Code, section 17602); EU consumers get the same terms before they order (Consumer Rights Directive, Articles 6(1) and 8(2)); and EU consumer sites must offer a "withdraw from contract here" function since 19 June 2026 (Consumer Rights Directive, Article 11a). Lenient return policies raised purchases more than returns, across 21 papers on retail goods (Janakiraman et al. 2016). Trials that need a card got about a third of the sign-ups of trials that don't in one agency's client data, and converted to paid 2.7 times as often (First Page Sage 2025); across about 200 business software products, more than 5 times as often (ChartMogul 2026). Top business products show compliance as text or links, such as "SOC 2 Type 2", in the footer, the plan table and a trust center, and most lead with a free plan rather than a guarantee (11-site survey, 2026, convention).
- **Applies when:** a page asks people to buy, subscribe, start a trial or book a sales call, except a waitlist or an early-access request, where nobody pays yet.
- **Check:** list the doubts buyers raise, from sales calls, support or reviews where they exist, for example the price after a trial, the card, cancelling, security or the tools it works with. Find each answer on the page: a missing one fails. For a trial or plan that renews, check that its cost, renewal and how to cancel show before the card form: missing fails. A risk reducer for a doubt the offer doesn't raise, such as "No card needed" under "Book a demo", fails.
- **Fail:**
  - "Start free trial", with the price after the trial and how to cancel shown only after the card form;
  - a security section that says "bank-grade security" with no standard or link.
- **Pass:**
  - under "Start free trial": "14 days free, no card needed. Then $12 a month; cancel anytime in Settings.";
  - "SOC 2 Type 2" in the footer, linking to the trust center;
  - no FAQ on a short page whose sections already answer the doubts;
  - no guarantee, when a free plan lets people try first.

### 11. Forms and prompts on marketing pages ask only for the next step

A sign-up, demo or waitlist form asks only what its next step needs, often one email field or a sign-in button first, and the rest later. After sending, its confirmation says what happens next and when. A sign-up prompt doesn't cover the page on arrival or while people read: it sits in the page or in a small banner. Agreeing to marketing messages is a separate box, never ticked in advance. In the EU, a cookie banner that asks for consent offers a way to refuse on the same layer as its accept button, not only as a link in its text.

- **Why:** Longer checkouts lose people: 17% of US shoppers abandoned one as too long or complicated, and testing suggests 7 to 8 fields are enough (Baymard, "Cart Abandonment Rate", 2026). 4 of 9 sales forms on top product sites open with one email field and ask the rest on the next step, and most sign-up pages open with a sign-in button or one email field (11-site survey, 2026, convention). A confirmation tells people what happens next and when (GOV.UK, "Confirmation pages"). Dialogs that cover a page frustrate people and cost their trust, a small banner does the same job, and only one the law requires, such as an age check, is exempt (Google Search Central, "Avoid intrusive interstitials and dialogs"). In the EU, silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity are not consent (GDPR, Recital 32), and most data protection authorities found no valid consent in a cookie banner with no refusal on the layer that has the accept button, or with a refusal only as a link in its text that nothing draws the eye to (EDPB, "Cookie Banner Taskforce", 2023). Without these, people agree to messages and tracking they never chose.
- **Applies when:** a page has a sign-up, demo, waitlist or contact form, a prompt to sign up, or a cookie banner.
- **Check:** list each field before the first send, and ask what the next step needs it for: a field it doesn't need fails. Look at every checkbox, from each region the product sells to: a consent box ticked in advance fails. Send the form, or read the code for its confirmation: one that doesn't say what happens next fails, and so does one that leaves out when, where the team knows it. Open the page in a fresh private window and read for a minute: a sign-up or discount dialog that covers the content fails. Open it the same way from the EU: a cookie banner with an accept button and no refusal on the same layer fails, and so does a refusal that is only a link in its text.
- **Fail:**
  - a demo form with nine required fields, phone and budget among them, before any reply;
  - a sign-up form whose newsletter box is ticked by default;
  - a discount pop-up that covers the page three seconds after it loads.
- **Pass:**

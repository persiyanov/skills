# Method Deep-Dive

Reference for the hard calls: scoring rubric, anchor mechanics, specificity, category strategy, diagnosis, and the founder-psychology patterns behind them. Sources: Fletch PMM (nearly 400 B2B positioning projects), April Dunford, Geoffrey Moore, and insights from 20+ operators (Raskin, Lochhead, Jackson, Omojola, Zhao, Vohra, and others). A few decision heuristics are this skill's own synthesis rather than sourced method, marked where they appear.

## The ICP scorecard rubric

Prerequisite: workflow-based segments already mapped (workflow, current alternative, felt problem). Score each 1 to 5.

**Problem severity: is there a compelling reason to buy?** The most important criterion. High: the problem costs them money, time, or deals now, and they know it. Switching energy already exists. Trend direction adds signal (Moore): a niche where the legacy solution is getting worse each year beats one where the pain is flat, because deterioration does the demand generation for you. Low: the alternative is good enough, and you'd have to educate them into caring. Education is possible but belongs in a demand-gen budget, not an ICP. A weak score signals the market may not exist yet for this segment.

**Differentiation: are we significantly better than their alternative?** Judge per segment, against that segment's alternative. The same product scores differently across segments. Even Notion lacks a strong edge in some segments. Strong positioning aims at order-of-magnitude better on a vector the segment cares about, the "monopolistic advantage." If it is weak everywhere, either narrow segmentation until an advantage appears or change the product or business model. Without a 10x edge growth tends to be linear rather than hyperbolic. That is acceptable, but say it out loud.

**Access: can we reliably get in front of them?** A 10x product for people you can't reach equals a bad product for people you can. Concretely: is there a channel (community, list source, event, keyword) you can work week after week? Proxy question: how confident is the user of hitting their numbers in this segment?

**Size: does it support the near-term revenue goal?** The least important criterion and the one founders over-weight. You are not validating unicorn TAM. You are checking the segment covers the next revenue milestone and burn. The early-stage goal is momentum, not revenue maximization.

Tie-breakers: severity beats size, and that ordering is Fletch's own. Access vs. differentiation is this skill's heuristic, not Fletch's: lean toward access for a first segment, because a team can deepen product faster than it can conjure a channel. But Fletch reads weak differentiation as a market-existence signal. If it is weak everywhere, no channel fixes that.

## Anchors: mechanics and traps

**Why primary anchors are mandatory.** They are the cognitive entry point. Without one, value statements have nothing to attach to. "Helps salespeople in small businesses save time" leaves the listener asking what the product is. Benefits, differentiation, and ROI all sit downstream of classification.

**Choosing among the three primary anchors:**
- **Category anchor**, when a well-understood category exists and being filed there helps. It imports the buyer's evaluation framework, shortlist, and budget line. Positioning inside an existing category with budgets usually beats inventing one (Todd Jackson). Trap: the category also imports expectations. If buyers will punish the product for missing table-stakes features, the category is premature.
- **Use-case anchor**, when the category would mis-frame the product, doesn't exist, or the market is immature and runs on tool stacks and manual processes rather than vendors. The default recommendation for early-stage B2B: it self-selects people doing the workflow and dodges feature-checklist comparisons. It is also the escape from the comparison trap (Maples): a radically different product should deny the incumbent's premise, not invite a feature-by-feature bake-off.
- **Competitive-alternative anchor**, when one incumbent is universally known and resented in the segment ("Slack is an email replacement"). Highest clarity, narrowest reach, invisible to anyone who doesn't know the incumbent. Works as SOM-level messaging even when the SAM-level anchor is category or use case.

**Specificity dial.** Each anchor has a vague and a specific form: "software company" vs. "CRM," "helps you run your business" vs. "helps you compose emails," "replaces traditional tools" vs. "an Arc replacement." Vague anchors are the most common failure in the wild. Test: does the anchor alone let a stranger file the product correctly?

**Anchor choice is segment choice.** "Clay alternative" reaches only Clay-aware buyers. "A way to enrich your CRM" reaches everyone with the task. Choose knowing who each anchor excludes.

**Layering.** One primary plus one or two secondary anchors is the clarity sweet spot: "a PMM consultancy [category] that helps marketers [department] in B2B software companies [company type] position their products [use case]." Stacked secondary anchors without a primary never add up to clarity.

**Horizontal products.** Position a flexible, horizontal tool inside a familiar category or leading use case anyway. Notion wears "productivity suite" and users discover the deeper power later. A "platform" message multiplies the theoretical audience while dividing resonance. Anchor early-stage messaging on one use case, prove traction, then rework if needed. Repositioning around a different use case is cheap. Being unintelligible is not.

## Specificity: revenue target, SAM/SOM, sequencing

The classic confusion: founders take $100M-vision positioning into markets where the goal is the first $1M to $5M. The revenue target is the constraint that makes the narrowing decision easy. Shopify's sequence: "for snowboarding stores" at the $1M goal, "for sporting-goods stores" at $10M, "for SMB e-commerce" at $50M and beyond. Sequencing records ambition instead of losing it.

Two simultaneous levels, both recorded in the artifact:
- **SAM-level:** intelligible across the few adjacent segments. Lives on the homepage, deck intro, broad content.
- **SOM-level:** precision messaging for one segment. Lives on use-case landing pages, persona campaigns, personalized sales material. Highest conversion and highest cost, so build it after the SAM level works and a segment shows traction.

## Category strategy

- Existing category: the default. Budgets, analysts, shortlists, and mental shelves already exist, so the job reduces to "obviously best for this segment."
- Subcategory, "X for Y": the cheap middle path. Inherits the shelf, claims a corner.
- Category design: the biggest prize (category kings take about two-thirds of the category's market cap, per Lochhead) and the most expensive: analyst validation, sustained thought leadership, educating buyers into a new budget line (Gago). Lochhead insists on the word design, not creation. "Creation" triggers product-feature thinking ("first to ship X features"), while the real work is market architecture: choosing the vocabulary and rules that make comparison to incumbents irrelevant. Category design is never the accidental result of vague language. A user saying "next-generation platform for digital transformation" is not designing a category. They are hiding from a shelf.
- Elevate a profession: instead of fighting for a shelf, elevate a professional identity, as Gainsight did by inventing "customer success" and making its practitioners a movement. A job is an identity people rally behind, while a category is a budget line. Works when the target role is underserved and hungry for recognition.
- Repositioning is a lever, not a failure. The same product can be dead in one category and take off in another: the "Microsoft Access killer" became an "embeddable database for mobile devices" and sales followed. If adoption stalls but retained users love the product, interview them and re-shelve around the use case where it already transforms their work.

## Narrative: shift, foil, movement

The strongest B2B narratives open with a shift in the world, old game to new game (Raskin, and Benioff's "No Software"). The pitch names what changed (technology, regulation, behavior), why the old way now loses, and presents the product as how you win the new game. Frame competitors as relics of the old game rather than out-featuring them. A concrete foil beats an abstract straw man: Square's "ugly old cash register," Kondo's LinkedIn inbox. Buyers must recognize the villain from their own life, so "legacy solutions" fails. A second, distinct narrative instrument: sell the transformation, not the utility. A saddle company that sells horseback riding grows the market for saddles, earns the perfect context to talk about its product, and positions itself as the leader of the activity rather than one vendor of the tool. The shift narrative needs a real change in the world. The transformation frame needs none, only an aspirational outcome the product genuinely enables. Use the shift when the world moved, the transformation when the ambition is bigger than the tool, both when you have both. A fabricated movement reads as marketing theater either way. And aim the story at what the audience already cares about (Meservey): changing someone's worldview is a huge lift, while shaping your story to fit their existing passions is a light one. Build the bridge from their interests to the product rather than asking them to cross from yours.

## Differentiation quality bar

- Anchor to the alternative. Features are only "differentiated" relative to what the segment uses today. Start from the alternative's real deficiencies, not from the feature list.
- One sharp value vector beats five mushy advantages: the single dimension where the product is provably superior and the segment cares. Speed for Superhuman, instant availability for Cash App. Different alone is easy and worthless. Better alone invites the better trap. The standard is better in a way that matters, provable now.
- Durability check: could the incumbent copy it in six months? If yes, it is a feature, not a moat. Still usable in messaging, but keep hunting for structural differentiation in business model, architecture, data, or distribution.
- Falsifiability: every claim should survive "prove it on a demo call." "10x faster queries on datasets under 1TB" survives. "Revolutionary AI" does not.
- In commodity markets, craft and emotional resonance are real differentiation (Linear, Arc). Then the product experience is the proof, and the messaging must let it show.

**Differentiation menu.** When the user is stuck naming a vector, walk this list. Strong companies combine 2 to 4: lowest price, highest quality, most convenient, best product experience, best customer service, best solution to a specific problem, limited or unique supply, most personalized, most delightful, most trusted, most flexible, good for the world, tribe/community, status, story. The honest alternative also exists: differentiate little and win on execution speed and scale, as Uber did against Lyft. That is a funding strategy rather than positioning, so name it if it is the real plan.

**The 2x2 check** ("Loserville," from the Foundation Sprint, outside the core sources but a useful test): put the two chosen differentiators on the axes of a 2x2 as customers perceive them. The product should sit alone in the top-right, with the alternatives forming an L in the other quadrants. If no honest pair of differentiators puts the product alone in the top-right, the differentiation isn't real yet. Go back to segment narrowing or product change instead of shipping the claim.

## Diagnosing positioning problems

Leading indicators. Listen to sales calls instead of waiting for churn.
- **False comprehension:** "oh, so you're like Salesforce." Filed on the wrong shelf.
- **Restart requests:** "back up, pitch it to me again."
- **"Why not a spreadsheet?"** Value against the status quo never landed.
- **Sluggish deals:** extra calls spent explaining what the product is.
- **Inconsistent internal narrative:** sales, product, and the founder describe the product differently. Most "positioning problems" at growth stage are alignment problems: the founder's working sales story was never codified. The fix is a cross-functional positioning exercise with the CEO in the room, not new copy.
- **Jargon test:** can a non-expert repeat what the product does after one explanation (Wiz's "dummy explanation")? Can a user pass the cocktail-party test?
- **The pitch gap:** compare how the founder describes the value with how the best customers describe it. When they differ, the customers' version is the truth. Rewrite the copy toward it rather than educating customers toward the pitch.

## Timing and honesty

- **Pre-PMF: stay flexible.** Run the session cheaply, mark it `Hypothesis`, and let market pull revise it. Invest in formal positioning once customer patterns repeat. Message-market fit can be tested before the product is finished: pitch the value and ask a willingness-to-pay question months before launch.
- **Safe messaging kills.** Consensus copy produced by committee is invisible. A provocative, specific stand that repels the wrong buyers is doing its job. Positioning that offends nobody and excludes nobody selects nobody.
- **Demand-gen reality check.** With positioning set, the fundamentals are boring on purpose: pick the specific segment, find the companies that plausibly have the problem, publish useful content about the problem where the segment hangs out, show up daily. Tools and growth hacks don't rescue an unmade positioning choice.

# Applying Positioning

How to turn the positioning artifact (`.agents/positioning.md`, or the root `POSITIONING.md` — see `SKILL.md`) into copy, campaigns, and product decisions. Read the artifact first. Every output must trace back to it. If the requested task pulls against the recorded bet ("write a homepage for everyone"), surface the conflict before writing. The user either adjusts the ask or you EVOLVE the artifact. Never resolve the conflict silently in the copy.

A request for a segment the artifact doesn't know ("write cold outreach for our new segment") triggers a mini-EVOLVE before any writing: build that segment's triple (workflow, alternative, problem), score it, record it in the artifact as promoted or parked, then write. Never produce segment copy for a segment the artifact has never scored. That is how positioning and outbound drift apart.

## The messaging taxonomy

Five levels, broadest to most concrete:
1. **Platform**: the whole offering. Avoid as an early-stage message.
2. **Products**: the sellable units.
3. **Use cases**: what someone accomplishes. The early-stage messaging home.
4. **Capabilities**: what the product lets a user do.
5. **Features**: what makes the capabilities possible.

Rule of thumb: message at use-case and capability level, prove at feature level, promise at first-order-benefit level. The higher the message, the bigger the theoretical audience and the weaker the resonance.

## Writing rules for any copy this skill produces

- **Clarity over cleverness.** If a line needs a second read, rewrite it. Jargon-test every sentence: a non-expert should be able to repeat what the product does.
- **Specificity over vagueness.** "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" beats "save time on your workflow." Numbers, named tools, concrete scenarios.
- **Customer language over company language.** Pull vocabulary from the artifact's words-to-use list. Don't invent branded feature names or category buzzwords the buyer doesn't use.
- **One idea per section.** Each block advances one argument. The page builds a single case: anchor, problem, differentiation, proof, ask.
- **Claims carry proof.** Every benefit line is falsifiable and, where possible, backed on the spot with a number, screenshot, named customer, or demoable moment from the artifact's proof slot.
- **Cut the slop.** No "revolutionize," "seamless," "supercharge," "unlock," "next-generation." A phrase that could sit on any competitor's site unchanged says nothing. Delete or sharpen it.
- **No feature enumerations where an outcome belongs.** "Provides AI, automation, and integrations" is a parts list, not a message. Features appear as proof under a capability, in plain language. They never fill a headline, a benefit slot, or the differentiation claim.
- **Take a stand.** Consensus copy is invisible. A specific position that repels the wrong buyers is doing its job. Copy that offends nobody and excludes nobody selects nobody.

## Homepage / landing page

The hero must answer, above the fold, in order:
1. **What is it?** The primary anchor, verbatim from the artifact.
2. **What does it replace?** The competitive alternative, named concretely.
3. **What makes it different or better?** The value vector.

Only after those three land do high-level benefits earn belief. The default startup hero ("Boost revenue," "streamline operations") answers a fourth question, business outcome, before the first three, and fails.

**Headline scan test.** Visitors read headlines only. Concatenate the page's headlines alone: do they explain what the company does? If not, restructure before polishing sentences. Headlines clarify with capabilities, body text closes with benefits. Lead with the capability, close with the benefit: "Automate what you're already doing, on the platforms you already use," then the time saved.

**First-order benefits only.** Claim the direct output ("get contact info without clicking through hundreds of sites"), not downstream dreams ("more leads, less effort"). Second and third-order claims are less believable and hide what the product does.

**Six hero elements** to compose from: target customer, problem, product category (the primary anchor in whatever form was chosen), capabilities, features, benefits. The first four live in the artifact. Features come from its differentiated-solve slot or, if thin there, from the product or repo at writing time, in plain language without branded feature names. A specific audience callout drives resonance. "For businesses" selects nobody.

**Page-type mapping:**
- Homepage and deck intro: SAM-level positioning.
- Use-case and persona landing pages: SOM-level. One page covers one segment's triple (workflow, alternative, problem), written in that segment's language.
- Early stage: anchor the whole homepage on the one chosen use case. Don't build a self-select multi-use-case homepage until one use case has proven traction.

## Cold email and outbound campaigns

- Target list = the ICP triple made operational: companies observably doing the workflow, ideally observably using the alternative (tech-detect, job posts, content signals). List quality is positioning quality.
- Open with the problem with their alternative, the pain they already feel, in their words. The artifact's problem statement is the first-line raw material, and its customer-language section supplies the vocabulary. Don't open with the product.
- One email, one segment, SOM-level. A "personalized" email whose value prop fits any recipient is SAM copy wearing a mail merge.
- Message on the use case rather than one buyer-specific outcome. Buyers arrive with different desired outcomes for the same workflow. The use case speaks to all of them, and sales tailors the outcome case per account in discovery.
- The anchor works in email too. One clause that files the product ("we're a [category]," "[incumbent] alternative") beats three sentences of unanchored value.
- Scale the CTA with problem severity from the scorecard. A severe felt problem takes a direct ask. A weaker one takes the useful-content, problem-education play, because that is demand creation, not capture.

## Sales pitch and deck

Dunford's two-part pitch structure, her most-tested framework:
1. **The setup, before the product appears.** Open with the unique market insight from the artifact (the value vector or Layer-3 wedge). Quality bar: no competitor could credibly claim the same insight. A generic problem statement fails it, and so does a market trend everyone can cite. Then walk the real alternatives with honest pros and cons, including the status quo. Close the setup with the perfect-world gate: "in a perfect world, the solution would do X and Y." Get agreement before showing anything.
2. **The follow-through.** Demo the product mapped point by point to the perfect world the buyer just agreed to, then prove it with a case study from the artifact's proof slot.
The deck intro carries SAM-level positioning, and the insight opens the pitch, never the product.

## Campaigns and content

- Demand capture targets the ICP: already doing the workflow, already feeling the problem. Demand creation targets those one condition short. Run creation deliberately with an education budget, never by accident.
- Content strategy comes straight from the artifact: publish about the problem and the workflow, in the channel where the segment already hangs out, on a steady cadence. Demonstrate expertise on the problem and make the engagement path obvious.
- If the artifact records a shift or foil narrative, it is the campaign backbone, built in Raskin's five steps: name the shift, raise the stakes (winners are already playing the new game and holdouts lose), define the promised land the new game leads to, name the obstacles standing in the way, and present the product's capabilities as the gifts that overcome them.
- Frame every story as a bridge from what the segment already cares about to the product. Changing an audience's worldview is a huge lift, and fitting your story into their existing passions is a light one.
- Content can also sell the transformation rather than the tool: publish about the activity and outcome the product enables (the horseback riding, not the saddle). This grows the segment's market and positions the product as the leader of the activity. Anchor product mentions inside that context.
- Every campaign brief opens by citing which segment it serves, SAM or a named SOM. A campaign that can't name its segment is a finding against the campaign, not a gap in the artifact.

## Product design, roadmap, and prioritization

Positioning is a decision filter, not only a marketing input. Evaluate any feature or roadmap question against the artifact:

1. **Deepens the bet.** Strengthens the value vector for the chosen segment or removes friction in the chosen workflow. Default yes. This work compounds the differentiation the messaging claims.
2. **Broadens the bet.** Serves the next segment in the recorded sequence, the Shopify move. Yes when the current segment shows traction and the artifact's sequencing names it next. Premature otherwise.
3. **Hedges the bet.** Serves a parked or rejected segment, or matches an incumbent feature outside the value vector, the better trap in roadmap form. Default no. A user who keeps wanting hedges is signaling an EVOLVE: re-score the segments instead of smuggling a pivot through the backlog.
4. **Table stakes.** Required by the chosen category anchor for credibility on the buyer's shelf. Yes, but timebox it. Parity work protects the anchor and never is the differentiation.

Additional lenses:
- **Craft as strategy.** If differentiation includes experience quality, papercut-fixing in the core workflow is first-rank roadmap work, not polish.
- **Proof-generating features.** Prefer work that makes the differentiation demoable, the try-to-send-me-a-dollar moment. It converts roadmap effort directly into messaging strength.
- **Effort is not message.** What was hard to build gets no messaging priority for that reason. Resonance with the segment's problem decides. This is the sunk-cost framing trap.
- When a better opportunity appears outside the bet, don't drift quietly. State it as a repositioning question, run EVOLVE, re-score, and update the artifact. Pivots are legal. Unrecorded drift is how positioning and product diverge.

## Keeping the artifact honest

After any APPLY task, check whether it surfaced new facts: a sharper customer phrase, a lost-deal reason, a competitor move. If yes, offer to fold them into the artifact (customer language, proof points). The artifact stays alive by being fed from real contact with the market.

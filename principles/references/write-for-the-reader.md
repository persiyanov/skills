# Write for the Reader

Write to change what the reader knows, believes, or does, not to report what you know or did. Readers owe you nothing and read for what it does for them. This applies to anything someone reads: product copy, a changelog, an email, a message, a doc, an API description, a prompt for an agent. Before the first word:

1. **The reader.** Who reads this most often, where they meet it (a screen, an inbox, a forward from their boss, a field in a reference), what they already know, what they use today, and what they want right now. A reader who does not know the problem needs the problem first; one who already uses the product needs what is new. Check this against real users and the data you can reach, not against imagination.
2. **The change.** What they should think or do after reading, in one sentence, and everything they need to do it. That sentence is the core message. If nothing changes for them, do not write it.
3. **Their value, their words.** Why that change matters to them, said as they would say it. Use the words they already use, never the names you invented while building, and never the mechanism they do not operate.
4. **Select.** Rank everything you could say by how much it moves the reader to the change. Keep what they need and nothing else, however hard it was to build. Every claim must be true: check each name, number, and promise.
5. **Order.** The core message first, then the support it needs, grouped. Each line must stand on its own where it is read.
6. **Read it fresh.** You know too much to read it as they will. Read it as the reader who was not there, or give it to someone who was not: what did they understand, what would they do, how did it make them feel, would a person they trust say it this way? If any answer is wrong, go back to step 2.

A format rule (a policy, a template, a length limit) shapes the text after these steps; it never decides what the text says. When it would cut something the reader needs, the rule gives way.

A correction from the user means a step was missed. Find which one and redo the text from there; never patch the sentence that was flagged.

Sources: Larry McEnerney's *The Craft of Writing Effectively* (value is the reader's; writing changes minds), Eugene Schwartz's levels of awareness, Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick* (the curse of knowledge, the core message), Amazon's Working Backwards press release, Barbara Minto's *Pyramid Principle*, and Paul Grice's maxims (as much as needed, true, relevant, clear).

Ask: Who reads this, what should they think or do after, and does every line move them there in their own words?

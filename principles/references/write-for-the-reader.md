# Write for the Reader

Write to change what the reader knows, believes, or does, not to report what you know or did. Readers owe you nothing, and they read for what the text does for them. This applies to anything that someone reads: product copy, a changelog, an email, a message, a doc, an API description, or a prompt for an agent.

Before the first word, settle three things:

1. **The reader.** Who reads this most often, where they meet it, what they already know, and what they want right now. Check this against real users and the data that you can reach, not against your imagination.
2. **The change.** What they should think or do after reading, in one sentence. That sentence is the core message. If nothing changes for them, don't write the text.
3. **Their value, in their words.** Why the change matters to them, said the way they'd say it, never in names that you invented while building.

For example, the changelog line "Refactored the send queue" reports the work. "Scheduled messages now go out within a minute, instead of up to ten" changes what the reader expects.

You know too much to read the text the way they will, so read it as the reader who wasn't there, or give it to someone who wasn't. If they'd misunderstand it or wouldn't act on it, go back to the change.

A format rule, such as a template or a length limit, shapes the text after these steps, but it never decides what the text says. When the rule would cut something that the reader needs, the rule gives way.

A correction from the user means that you skipped a step. Find which one, and redo the text from there instead of patching the sentence that they flagged.

For how to select, order, and write the text itself, use the writing skill.

Sources: Larry McEnerney, *The Craft of Writing Effectively* (the value is the reader's, and writing changes minds). Eugene Schwartz's levels of awareness. Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (the curse of knowledge, and the core message). Amazon's Working Backwards press release. Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*. Paul Grice's maxims (as much as needed, true, relevant, and clear).

Ask: Who reads this, what should they think or do after, and does every line move them there in their own words?
